# Wolf — Historic Lore: General Knowledge

**MERP-ui · English (British) · Five RMU outcomes · Translation of the original 2.0.17 RollTable results 231–235**

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

The darkest tales of the wars of the North have left you certain of one thing: the wolves that roam Eriador today are descended from animals once bred for the Enemy's armies. According to this tradition, the servants of Angmar released packs trained to track Men into the woods and moors, so that their vigilance might survive the disappearance of the garrisons. Some families still tell how a beast glimpsed near an ancient military road retains the instincts of its forebears and can recognise, by scent alone, the descendants of those who fought the Witch-king. You learnt this story as a warning so ancient that its age seems proof enough. Consequently, the presence of a wolf near ruins or abandoned roads appears to you to reveal a secret loyalty to the Shadow, whose old alliances endure long after their masters have vanished.

### Failure — `failure`

In stories handed down from the campaigns against Angmar, wolves frequently appear in the wake of armed Men. Fireside accounts speak of howling around bivouacs, tracks discovered after battles, and beasts seen amongst the remains left along the roads. You were taught to read such references as signs of a constant relationship between wolf packs and the movements of troops: wherever soldiers pass, wolves will eventually appear, and their presence often foretells the imminent arrival of an enemy warband. This interpretation is reinforced by tales of watchmen who heard howling on the heights and saw an armed column emerge in the following days. You therefore know that any such encounter deserves to be reported to the sentries: in your eyes, it is amongst the oldest and surest warnings known to those who live along the frontiers.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

The chronicles of the northern kingdoms speak of wolves long before the wars against Angmar burdened their image with any particular menace. They already belonged to the woods, moors and mountains, where hunters, travellers and herdsmen had to reckon with their presence. Later, memories of raids and sieges brought ordinary wolves, Wargs and other beings associated with the power of the Shadow into the same accounts. You know that these testimonies do not necessarily describe the same creatures. A wolf's appearance in a wartime tale may reflect human fear, the abandonment of farmland or the proximity of carrion, as readily as a genuine threat. The historian's first precaution is therefore to place each account in its own age, without passing the reputation of monsters from ancient battles on to every beast living in the present.

### Success — `success`

The memory of the wars of the North cannot be read as a uniform description of the wilderness. The annals of a watchpost, the testimony of a shepherd driven from his grazing lands and the account of a warrior returned from defeat preserve different observations, even when each mentions wolves. You know that armed bands could attract predators through camp refuse and carcasses abandoned in their wake; such proximity implied neither obedience nor alliance. Conversely, some traditions describe Wargs and malicious creatures whose actions cannot be explained by the habits of the ordinary wolf alone. To understand a testimony, you examine the actions attributed to the beasts, any apparent co-ordination with Orcs, the place of their appearance and the distance separating the narrator from the events. Such inquiry does not lessen the horror of ancient wars; it merely prevents those horrors from becoming a law applied to every encounter in the woods of Eriador.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You know that the history of wolves in Middle-earth unfolds upon several levels which it would be imprudent to confuse. The ancient tales of Beleriand preserve memories of monstrous wolves and beings subject to dark powers; accounts from later ages describe Wargs and the terrors of the wars of the North. Yet in inhabited countryside, generations of hunters and shepherds have also encountered wolves without those animals serving any master's designs. These sources differ both in age and purpose: a heroic song magnifies an encounter with evil, a chronicle seeks to preserve the memory of peril, and a village's recollections often tell of the cost of a difficult winter. You distinguish beings explicitly bound to the Shadow, animals whose presence happens to accompany armies, and images storytellers use to portray desolation. Such critical reading does not diminish the power of legends; it reveals what they truly say without imposing upon a living wolf the part played by another in the memory of the world.
