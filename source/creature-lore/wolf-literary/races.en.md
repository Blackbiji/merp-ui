## Men (Hildor)

*Specialisation: Men (Hildor) · Family: Racial Lore — Major Peoples · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

Among Men, the wolf never belongs entirely to the world of beasts alone. It haunts pastures, winter roads and the stories told within households, yet also serves as an image of hunger, solitude, loyalty and the violence of the powerful. The memories of the peoples of Eriador cannot, however, be mistaken for those of the herdsmen of Rohan, the hunters of the North or the inhabitants of the southern lands. This general knowledge concerns the ways human communities learn to regard the wolf, protect those in their care and pass on their experiences. It does not ascribe identical customs to all the Hildor, nor does it replace the lore of any particular culture or region.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

You have been taught that a wolf which follows the lights of a house for several nights in succession has chosen to return until one member of that household is surrendered to it. There would be no point in moving the flocks or repairing the fences: until somebody from the house has confronted it, the beast will recognise the place and sooner or later bring its companions there. This belief feeds upon stories in which a missing animal, a season of hardship and a howl heard behind the barns become successive episodes in a single relentless pursuit.

The oldest version of the tale holds that such beasts can distinguish one dwelling from another, even amongst neighbouring houses. They supposedly recognise the paths taken by its inhabitants, wait beyond the firelight and abandon their pursuit only after an open confrontation. Shepherds who have inherited the story therefore advise against wasting time moving the flocks: the wolf would find them again until the offence which drew it there had been redressed.

### Failure — `failure`

You believe you know a rule widely repeated amongst keepers of livestock: a wolf which has approached human beings without attacking them must have lost its fear and become harmless. It is said that a truly dangerous beast would reveal its intentions at the first encounter, whereas one which remained silent beside a road has accepted the presence of people. This comforting distinction, often repeated as the counsel of an old shepherd, appears to offer a means of judging a creature in an instant.

Those who pass on the rule cite encounters in which a wolf withdrew without a sound before a motionless traveller. They explain that an animal intent upon attacking would already have advanced, while one which allowed a man to pass has accepted his presence in the country. Travellers are consequently advised to use the same tone of voice and gestures during later encounters, for the wolf will supposedly recognise the person it once allowed to go unharmed.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that human peoples relate to wolves differently according to their livelihoods. Where sheep, goats and horses spend the warmer months far from settled dwellings, the protection of young animals and the gathering of the herd at dusk are matters of particular importance. In hunting lands, people are more concerned with wolves amongst deer or animals already weakened. Town-dwellers often know the creature through markets, travellers' tales and warnings brought in from the farms. The fear is real, but neither its strength nor its causes are the same everywhere.

You can also distinguish the animal from the tales told about it. A pack may follow a road without lying in wait for travellers; howling may serve to maintain contact between separated individuals rather than to announce an attack. A lone wolf is not necessarily the leader of an unseen band, any more than the sight of several wolves proves that they intend to pursue human beings. Communities living near predators first learn to guard their livestock, watch the approaches and avoid leaving food that might draw the animals towards their homes.

### Success — `success`

You understand why human stories of wolves so often take the form of moral warnings. In a household where several generations have known famine, the beast prowling beyond the fire embodies hunger; in an isolated village, it is a reminder not to travel alone along snowbound roads; to a stockbreeder who has lost his animals, it assumes the shape of a stubborn enemy. Such figures have power because they give a face to wider dangers. Yet they do not describe a purpose shared by all wolves, and the same behaviour may receive opposite interpretations on either side of a frontier.

You also know the difference between measures which genuinely protect a herd and gestures intended merely to ward off fear. Gathering vulnerable animals together, posting guards, keeping refuse under control and choosing a suitable resting place matter more than a threat shouted into the night. Hedges and fences may restrict an approach, but they are not absolute boundaries; their effectiveness depends upon the ground, their upkeep and the routes available nearby. When a wolf returns to the same valley, the repeated visits may indicate an accessible food source or a convenient passage rather than the resentment with which popular stories burden it.

Finally, you recognise how profoundly the wars of the North have altered certain human traditions. Natural wolves may have been confused with larger, more hostile creatures possessed of intentions foreign to their kind. When a witness describes a beast that seemed to understand the commands of an enemy, you do not dismiss the account without examination; neither do you attribute that singular behaviour to every animal encountered thereafter. Membership of the race of Men offers access to an abundant memory of danger, loss and precaution; it does not in itself confer certain knowledge of what left a footprint in the mud.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You can read human traditions as a long history of the relationship between settlements, worked lands and places that have grown wild once more. When a kingdom maintains its roads, protects its herds and supports numerous farms, the opportunities available to wolves differ from those found in a country ravaged by war. An abandoned road may become overgrown; a valley once given over to grazing may again attract wild herbivores; a boundary separating inhabited estates may cease to mean anything to animals. Chronicles sometimes preserve the memory of an age when wolves seemed absent, followed by the story of their return. You know that this succession does not necessarily reveal any new intention on the part of the predators, but often reflects changes in human settlement, wild game and access to food.

You distinguish the sources of an account. A watchman describes an attack he witnessed; his son recalls a season in which livestock became scarce; a passing storyteller speaks of a monster that fastened upon the village. Their words may spring from the same event while conveying very different kinds of information. You seek details which can be checked: the hour, season, number of beasts lost, condition of the fences, presence of snow, bite marks and tracks. You lend more weight to corroborating observations than to the reputation of a place, without despising the customs which helped its inhabitants survive difficult winters.

You also know that a community may blame wolves for losses caused by something else. A sheep found dead beside a wood may have succumbed to disease before a scavenger fed upon it; tracks pressed into snow may belong to several separate passages; a missing young animal may have inspired a story all the more confident because nobody actually witnessed what happened. Conversely, the absence of visible marks on stony ground does not rule out the passage of a predator. This caution does not lead you to absolve wolves as a matter of principle: it helps you recognise when the evidence truly supports attributing an attack to them.

Finally, you understand why knowledge of wolves and knowledge of the Shadow must remain separate. During certain wars, great canids may have accompanied malevolent forces and left a lasting memory. Yet a deep footprint, a howl near a ruin or a carcass discovered on an old road proves no alliance. Men have often bestowed upon the animals of their tales the passions and loyalties they recognise in one another. Your skill lies in identifying this transposition, preserving what it reveals of dangers past, and recovering beneath the fable the observable realities of the present. It enables you to interpret a shepherd's, hunter's or veteran's words fairly without treating any one experience as a universal law.

## Dúnedain

*Specialisation: Dúnedain · Family: Racial Lore — Major Peoples · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

Among the Dúnedain, knowledge of the wolf may be passed down through estate archives, reports from guards, the recollections of trackers and accounts of the wars of the North. The heirs of the Númenóreans have preserved, according to region and age, different ways of watching the roads and describing the lands under their protection. The memory of Arnor is not that of Gondor, and an ancient chronicle can never take the place of examining a living country. This table concerns the Dúnadan manner of ordering testimony, establishing precautions and distinguishing natural danger from memories of the Shadow. It describes no inborn understanding of wolves supposedly shared by every descendant of Númenor.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

A tradition ascribed to the ancient captains of Arnor has convinced you that no natural wolf willingly crosses the boundary stones of a royal highway. These stones were supposedly raised with a craft that kept wild beasts beyond the inhabited estates, and predators are said still to recognise the boundary long after the guards have disappeared. Accounts tell of pursuits abruptly broken off beside a bridge or an upright marker, as though the memory of the kingdom endured within the animals themselves.

It is said that the old surveyors never laid out an important road without setting such protective markers in the ground. Those along the northern marches supposedly retained their virtues even when the names of the kings had faded. A traveller who recognised the carved signs could therefore find upon an abandoned highway the same safety once offered by a guard stationed at its far end. He need only remain on the proper side of the boundary to force a pursuing pack to turn back.

### Failure — `failure`

You have learnt that a report compiled by several guards can always establish the exact number of wolves in a pack from the tracks converging upon a road. Every sequence of footprints supposedly has its place in the count, and the old Dúnedain methods are said to distinguish the tracks of each animal without any possibility of error. This confidence in records kept by watchposts seems more dependable to you than the uncertain testimony of a shepherd.

The registers of ancient guardposts appear to confirm the method. Sometimes they contain lists of individual marks, each accompanied by an indication of size or direction. Tradition holds that Dúnadan trackers could assign them unhesitatingly to separate animals, even where their footprints overlapped. You might therefore assume that an apparently meticulous old survey gives the number of wolves with an accuracy comparable to a count of soldiers.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that the Dúnedain watched over roads, bridges, settlements and frontiers for many years, and that maintaining them demanded an exact knowledge of the dangers posed by the land. Natural wolves were amongst those dangers whenever herds or game drew them near inhabited estates. Watchmen might record an encounter, note the state of a crossing or warn travellers about animals in the hills. These observations belonged to the ordinary life of a country; they did not, in themselves, establish any intervention by the Shadow.

You also know that chronicles never describe an unchanging landscape. Where villages have emptied and watchposts vanished, the roads have altered and animals have found other ways through. An old reference to wolves beside a bridge tells you something of the concerns of that age; it guarantees neither their presence there now nor their absence elsewhere. Thus the lore handed down amongst the Dúnedain joins the memory of ancient ways to the examination of recent evidence.

### Success — `success`

You know how a good watchman distinguishes what his report records from the interpretation he places upon it. Tracks found near a tower, a silhouette glimpsed at dawn and howling heard from a valley are not necessarily three pieces of evidence for the same pack. Careful sentinels record the time and place, the state of the ground and the possibility that tracks have been obscured or used repeatedly. They also distinguish losses demonstrably caused by predators from those for which no cause was observed. A report's value lies less in the rank of its author than in the precision of what he records.

You understand how the wars against Angmar sometimes clouded this discipline. Tales of great wolves associated with armed bands entered the kingdom's memory, and it became tempting to read a hostile purpose into every nocturnal appearance. Such caution, inherited from wartime, may be justified when a genuine enemy is at work; it must not, however, turn a wolf following game into an enemy scout. You give Wargs their proper place in accounts where their nature appears established, without extending their supposed powers to ordinary wolves.

Finally, you know that keeping roads safe rests upon often modest practical measures: maintaining clear approaches, avoiding carcasses near stopping places, preventing young animals from becoming separated from the herd, and warning neighbouring villages when an attack has been confirmed. An old highway may allow people to travel faster, but it may also guide animals. Bridges and fords attract creatures that need to cross a river; their importance springs from the ground itself, not from any mysterious loyalty wolves might feel towards the kingdom's works.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You can place Dúnadan memories of wolves within the changing history of the kingdoms of North and South. Maintaining a garrison, occupying farms, opening a road or losing its guards alters the lives of the animals that frequent the same country. In Arthedain while it was still defended, wolves might keep away from a busy place yet use neighbouring valleys; amongst estates abandoned after the wars, those same approaches might become quieter and more favourable to game. Archives describing these situations do not necessarily contradict one another: they concern different periods. You know better than to turn a predator's presence into an automatic symptom of political decline, as though nature had waited for a kingdom to fall before reclaiming the land.

You understand the limits of written precision. A patrol may measure the depth of a footprint, record the spacing of tracks and sketch a trail without being able to determine an animal's weight with certainty. Rain may have broadened an outline, frost altered its shape, or several wolves followed the same course by stepping in one another's tracks. A series of matching reports becomes more persuasive when their places, dates and possible movements agree. Conversely, the apparent exactitude of figures copied from register to register adds nothing to an observation whose origins are uncertain.

You also know the political uses to which a chronicle may be put. A lord seeking to justify maintaining an outpost may dwell upon the dangers of neighbouring lands; a defeated officer may blame malevolent beasts for a rout caused by poor reconnaissance; a village requesting protection may gather several unrelated animals or incidents beneath the word 'wolves'. These witnesses need not be despised, but one must understand why some facts were recorded and others omitted. The silence of an archive on wolves does not demonstrate their absence, especially when the guards had graver threats to report.

Finally, you know how to bring ancient memory and present observation together. The Dúnedain may recognise on a map or in a story the passes commanding entry to a valley; nevertheless, today's fords, overgrown tracks and movement of livestock must be checked. You distinguish a tradition of vigilance from direct experience and from what the great wars invested with dread. A wolf's silhouette beside a highway has no political intention; it becomes useful intelligence when placed amongst the movements of game, available resources and neighbouring observations. This discipline of judgement, rather than any secret art of the ancient kings, is the finest inheritance of Dúnadan lore.

## Elves (Quendi)

*Specialisation: Elves (Quendi) · Family: Racial Lore — Major Peoples · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

The Elves have known the forests, rivers and mountains for ages beyond the grasp of mortal memory. Yet this long acquaintance with Middle-earth does not make all the Quendi alike: the traditions of the Nandor, Sindar, Avari, Noldor and Teleri differ in both the landscapes they inhabit and the songs they sing. The natural wolf may appear in their tales as a voice in the night, a neighbour of the glades or a sign of change within the living world. Memories of war have also clothed it in characteristics properly belonging to Wargs and other creatures of the Shadow. This table concerns how to approach those memories without confusing ancient sensitivity, close observation and the mistaken notion of universal dominion over beasts.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

You have been taught that every natural wolf immediately recognises an Elf's voice and ceases to pose a danger as soon as it is addressed in one of the tongues of the Quendi. Even a starving or wounded beast would turn away from travellers capable of speaking a few words in the ancient language. This certainty springs from tales in which animals stopped beneath the trees when a singer raised his voice; the beauty of the story eventually transformed a singular encounter into a law of the world.

The oldest accounts maintain that this recognition depends not upon the meaning of the words, but upon the very sound of the Elven tongues. A traveller who remembers a verse learnt in childhood could thus halt a beast in its path, while the presence of a single Elf amongst a company would bring the same tranquillity to all. For this reason, some families pass down phrases to recite when howling draws nearer, teaching that a predator will hear the voice before it sees the traveller.

### Failure — `failure`

You know a belief according to which wolves dwelling in a forest frequented by Elves gradually cease hunting the creatures living there. The peace of the woodland would make them abandon the pursuit of deer; thereafter they would feed only upon animals already dead, while wolves in foreign lands retained their ordinary ferocity. Songs describing a wood in which no blood was spilt are cited as evidence that this transformation takes place naturally.

It is said that woods long protected by the Quendi possess a peace unknown to ordinary forests. Hunters have supposedly watched wolves follow deer without troubling them, then return to the remains of animals that died of old age. Tradition holds that wolves encountered amongst protected trees cannot suddenly resume hunting. If beasts there attacked living prey, they would necessarily have come from foreign lands or be creatures of another nature.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that Elven traditions distinguish the living beings of the natural world from creatures whose presence is bound to the powers of the Shadow. Ordinary wolves may be part of the balance of a forest or the neighbouring highlands, while Wargs in tales of war are capable of acts that should not be attributed to every animal of similar outline. Songs often preserve this distinction, but their images may be repeated without due care by storytellers of another age or tongue.

You know how to observe without assuming that ancient knowledge renders an animal tame. Wolves follow game trails, frequent woodland edges and choose according to the season the passages where they find food and shelter. A pack may move through the forest without any Elf having summoned or driven it away. Hearing a song beneath the trees does not prove that a wolf drew near to listen; it may have followed a trail or merely crossed ground that it already frequented.

### Success — `success`

You know the place held by patient observation in Elven accounts of the living world. Human generations may watch a wood change over a handful of decades; the memory of the Quendi can bring together periods vastly further apart. A path abandoned by deer may be used again, a clearing overgrown with trees, or a valley left without game after some great disturbance. The behaviour of wolves changes with such transformations. You know better than to apply the memory of a more abundant forest without examination to the one through which you now travel.

You also understand how much the differences between Elven peoples matter. Those who live beside the waters, those who frequent deep woods and those who retain the memory of cities and wars will not describe identical encounters. To some, the wolf is chiefly a creature heard far away; to others, a predator whose tracks must be recognised upon hunting paths; to others still, the recurring name of beasts that accompanied enemies. Such diversity forbids attributing a single doctrine on how to behave before a wolf to all the Quendi.

Tales of the Shadow demand particular care. A beast larger than usual, the memory of an army or a howl upon an old battlefield does not suffice to identify a Warg. Instead, you seek the behaviour actually witnessed: unusual coordination with warriors, signs of understanding beyond ordinary animal conduct, and testimony corroborated by independent observers. There is nothing exceptional about a pack hunting together; a pack seemingly carrying out a purpose unrelated to its search for food raises different questions.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You know that the long reach of Elven memory is both a treasure and a responsibility. It permits the disappearance of a path, the growth of a forest or the coming of new inhabitants to be compared with events known to other peoples only through stories. Yet it may also preserve the prestige of an ancient image long after the world it described has changed. A hunting song composed in the highlands of old proves nothing, by itself, about the present condition of a valley. You know how to recover the setting of a tradition, recognise changes in the landscape and judge what truly remains comparable from one age to another.

You understand that wolves belong to a web of relationships far wider than their proximity to Elves. The presence of large herbivores, harsh winters, the movements of Men, the opening of clearings and the safety of dens influence the places they frequent. When a pack disappears from a territory, the reason may be a decline in prey or pressure from other animals or hunters. Its return need not signal a blessing, any more than its departure marks a defilement. The wolf is neither the moral judge of a forest nor an intruder in every Elven domain.

You also know what the sounds of the night can and cannot establish. Howling signals a presence and maintains bonds between individuals; echoes in a valley, wind direction and obstacles in the landscape can distort its apparent source. A call alone indicates neither the number of wolves nor their intentions nor their relationship to another group. Tales describing answers from several hills may preserve a true event, yet their truth cannot be measured by the precision one imagines hearing in a song. Sound judgement requires examining tracks, movements and the resources that make a presence possible.

You further distinguish the beauty of a spoken word from its authority over living creatures. In adventures of the Elder Days there may have been singular meetings between Elves and certain creatures; their possible existence does not turn every wild beast into an obedient subject. The skill of a tracker, the patience of a woodland-dweller and respect for an animal's distance count for more than blind faith in the prestige of a language. Deep knowledge of the living world also means accepting that a creature may retain its own will and needs without seeking either to serve or to defy those watching it.

Finally, you can place the great wolf-stories within the wars from which they arose. The name 'Warg' may sometimes have been used precisely, but it may also have been extended to a more ordinary enemy through fear, translation or the passage of a tale from one people to another. You do not confuse the traces of living nature with the memory of hostile powers, nor do you allow either to efface the other. A natural wolf remains a hunter, sometimes fearful and sometimes dangerous according to circumstance; the Warg belongs to tales where intelligence, malice and ties to the Shadow demand another interpretation. Maintaining this distinction without dogmatism honours both the memory of the Quendi and the present reality of the forests.

## Khazâd (Naugrim)

*Specialisation: Khazâd (Naugrim) · Family: Racial Lore — Major Peoples · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

The Dwarves established their dwellings in the mountains, but their lives do not end at the doors of the great halls. Caravans cross the passes, scouts range over scree slopes, miners work in open quarries and travellers pass through valleys where wolves may leave their tracks. In the traditions of the Naugrim, the animal is often associated with long winter roads, threatened pack animals and dangers awaiting those who leave the safety of built works. The six Houses preserve different experiences; what unites them is less an inborn relationship with the wolf than an attentiveness to approaches, provisions and the security of crossings. The general lore described here belongs neither to any one House nor to an art of recognising every animal by the sound of its claws upon stone.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

An old caravanners' tale has taught you that wolves never pursue their prey beyond a threshold carved by Dwarves. Worked stone supposedly bears the mark of your people's craft, which wild beasts recognise as an impassable boundary. It is said that reaching the first step of a stairway, the lintel of an abandoned structure or a carved roadside stone is enough to bring a pursuit to an end.

A saying frequently recurs in the counsel of roadmasters: 'Reach the worked stone, and the pursuit is over.' It is associated with tales of caravans that narrowly escaped packs beside ancient gates and then credited their survival to the thresholds they had crossed. Dwarves who preserve this memory claim that even a solitary marker beside a mountain pass retains the same virtue, provided a craftsman of their people erected it according to the proper rules.

### Failure — `failure`

You accept as certain a rule attributed to old prospectors: a wolf whose claws ring distinctly upon a stone path is heavier and more dangerous than another, while the number of impacts heard in an open passage allows one to count approaching animals. Masters of echoes supposedly learnt to recognise the tread of each paw even while the beasts remained out of sight.

The most confident guides maintain that every animal has a recognisable cadence and that stone preserves it even through winding passages. They teach young caravanners to count the reverberations and distinguish the heavy steps of the most fearsome beasts. A succession of rapid impacts would announce several lightly built hunters, whereas deep, widely spaced knocks would betray a large animal. By this method, one could supposedly decide whether to open a door or continue along the road without ever seeing the pack.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that wolves frequent slopes inhabited by their prey and valleys linking grazing grounds to higher country. They may use the passes taken by herds and caravans without seeking to enter underground halls. Their presence near an abandoned entrance depends upon food, shelter and the movements of animals: a monumental doorway neither naturally attracts nor repels a pack.

The tales of the Naugrim chiefly teach one to recognise the dangers of a halt. Pack animals left away from fires and watchmen may attract predators; a carcass abandoned near a road may encourage them to return. Dwarves know the value of narrow ground for defending a caravan, but also understand that a confined space may become a trap when a heavily laden company has no room to withdraw. Stone protects only through the shape of the structure and those who defend it.

### Success — `success`

You understand the importance of the seasons to routes crossing the mountains. Snow may drive game into lower valleys; frost may make a pass easier to use or more perilous; melting snow swells the torrents and forces both people and animals towards a few fords. Wolves need not follow built roads to profit from these constraints. A pack may use a parallel ravine and reappear near the only practicable crossing, not through any secret knowledge of Dwarven architecture, but because geography draws several species towards the same places.

You know how to inspect the approaches to a road without reading hostile purpose into every track. A print in fresh snow, repeated passage near a watering place and the remains of game may indicate animal activity; they do not necessarily establish that a caravan is being followed. Natural wolves pursue prey, travel with their family groups and often avoid costly confrontations. Prudent drovers guard their pack animals, do not leave food unattended and identify possible exits before making camp.

Finally, you distinguish wolf-stories from accounts of warfare involving Orcs and Wargs. A pack of ordinary wolves may cross an Orc band's trail without becoming its ally; beasts drawn by scraps show no military discipline. Dwarven accounts rightly remember formidable enemies encountered along their marches, but prudence demands that the intelligence and malignity of those wartime creatures not be ascribed to every large canid.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You can compare the accounts of caravans by placing them back upon the routes from which they arose. A merchant forced to wait three days behind a snowbound pass will not describe the same encounters as a scout who crossed the high slopes at the beginning of summer. Watering places, rocky shelters, wooded areas and corridors used by game matter more than the boundaries between old Dwarven lordships. You recognise the danger of travellers' tales which attribute to an entire locality what happened in one exceptional season. A gorge may have been feared during a winter of famine and remained quiet for many years afterwards.

You understand how constructed works alter, without abolishing, the movements of living creatures. A closed wall may turn aside a predator; an open stairway will not do the same. A collapsed gallery may provide temporary shelter to animals, but tracks beside its entrance do not imply that a pack dwells in the deep halls. Wolves need practicable routes, opportunities to hunt and territory through which to move. Inhabited spaces, smoke, doors and worksites subject them to disturbances they may try to avoid. Thus you can distinguish the occasional use of a ruin from the lasting occupation of a site.

You also know the pitfalls of reading tracks upon stone. Bare rock retains few marks, while mud collected at the foot of a slope may preserve exaggerated prints. Scratches or caught hairs are not always enough to distinguish a wolf from other creatures. Wind blowing through a narrow passage amplifies some noises and masks others. One witness may swear to having heard six animals when the same footsteps echoed between two walls; another may notice nothing despite the presence of recent tracks. You therefore compare physical signs, the time of observation and possible movements before reaching a conclusion.

Finally, you know why the Houses' traditions may differ without any of them necessarily being false. Those who travelled through wooded mountains possess more tales of encounters near game; those who maintained routes through barren country chiefly remember threats to pack animals. A belief born of one attack may remain attached to a gate or pass long after it has lost all practical significance. The common knowledge of the Khazâd consists in placing such memories within the reality of passes, worksites and wildlife. You do not expect a stone to make a wolf retreat: you examine what the stone permits, what it obstructs and what the mountain offers to predator and traveller alike.

## Hobbits (Periannath)

*Specialisation: Hobbits (Periannath) · Family: Racial Lore — Major Peoples · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

Among Hobbits, wolves often belong to stories told when the fire burns in the hearth and the roads grow treacherous. The Periannath generally prefer cultivated lands, well-kept hedges, familiar paths and the company of neighbours, yet their forebears crossed countries where forest and cold held greater sway. They have preserved accounts of terrible winters, travellers far from villages and beasts glimpsed along the borders of inhabited lands. Harfoots, Fallohides and Stoors do not all observe the same customs. This table concerns their shared memory of caution, neighbourliness and the boundary between the familiar world and the wild beyond, without treating fireside tales as zoology.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

An old story has led you to believe that wolves cannot enter a country so long as all its hedges remain properly tended. Thorns, ditches and fences supposedly form a sort of ring recognised by the beasts, so that a road bordered by gardens and orchards would be perfectly safe even far from any dwelling. The tale explains that the first incursions of wolves always followed a neglected hedge, as though forgetting a garden were enough to open the way.

Families who care for their gardens readily quote this precept when a traveller asks where to sleep. They recommend a barn surrounded by thorns rather than an exposed height, even if the path to the barn passes through woodland. Every repaired hedge supposedly prolongs the country's safety; the first gap left unattended would be the place where wolves sought to return. Thus tending the land would offer protection almost as certain as a stone wall.

### Failure — `failure`

A saying handed down amongst Hobbit travellers assures you that a wolf which does not howl before nightfall is well fed and incapable of pursuing prey until dawn. Dangerous beasts would always announce themselves with long cries, while a silent wood would indicate a peaceful road. It is said that cautious walkers wait for the final call before deciding where to pitch their tents.

Travellers who follow this custom listen to the hills in the last light. If the cries have ceased, they reckon the beasts have finished hunting and they can cross the moor before the night is too far advanced. ### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that Hobbits have chiefly learnt to protect themselves through shared vigilance and care for inhabited places. You also know that fear of wolves has sometimes grown alongside fear of forests, bad weather and great distances. ### Success — `success`

Stories of severe winters, when preserved by several families, reveal the difficulty of the roads, the disappearance of game and the alarm caused by animals venturing near dwellings. They cannot, however, establish how many wolves lived in a country or prove that a new appearance must herald the return of some former calamity. Elders may have woven several years of poor harvests and separate encounters into one story.

You know that inhabited country is protected by modest habits: accompanying the young when they leave villages, gathering domestic animals, securing outbuildings and alerting neighbours to a presence actually observed. Finally, you know that Hobbits sometimes distinguish poorly between ordinary wolves and the malevolent creatures described by travellers from the North. When a tale gives a great wolf speech, schemes or loyalty to Orcs, you recognise that it belongs to a different kind of story. The experience of villages does not justify attributing such intelligence to every animal met in the fields. Common sense lies in remembering genuine dangers without allowing childhood fears to take the place of observation.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You can place the stories of the Periannath within the history of their migrations and settlements. A tale born near the highlands may survive in a country of gardens and quiet roads; its words remain unchanged, but those who hear it imagine a different landscape. You recognise these shifts of meaning and refuse to treat inherited fear as a certain description of the place where you stand. The absence of wolves from the everyday experience of a village does not make it impossible for them to pass through during an exceptional winter.

You distinguish the moral strength of a story from its observable content. When a young Hobbit wanders from the road and hears a howl, a tale remembered from home may prompt him to rejoin his companions; that is a useful lesson even if the wolf was not following him. Another story warns that provisions should be shared or that a traveller ought not to be left alone in bitter cold. These are teachings about communal life. To judge the danger posed by an animal, you look instead for tracks, the freshness of snow, the whereabouts of game, passages between woods and fields and the observations of people who actually saw the beasts.

They are excellent testimony to the priorities of Hobbits but less reliable records of the predators' behaviour. He despises neither popular memory nor the wild world. He merely knows that a distance lies between them which no story, however well told, excuses him from measuring.

## Orcs (Yrch)

*Specialisation: Orcs (Yrch) · Family: Racial Lore — Major Peoples · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

Among Orcs, wolf-stories arise on the marches, around lairs and beside fires where a band's refuse accumulates. The Yrch know that hunger draws predators back to scraps, that mountains conceal dangers besides their enemies, and that certain great canids have served the forces of the Shadow in war. But Orc bands share neither a single master nor a single experience of animals. The dominance claimed by captains is often a way of asserting power rather than a faithful description of the wild. This table describes the general knowledge an Orc may acquire in such surroundings, without repeating the particular traditions of common Orcs or confusing natural wolves with Wargs.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

You have repeatedly been told that any wolf which tastes flesh discarded by an Orc band thereafter belongs to those who fed it. Old raiders claim that the first mouthful imposes enduring loyalty: the beast will return when the warriors call, recognise their enemies and guard their camp without ever being trained. One need only throw meat beyond the ring of fires to acquire unseen allies.

Captains teach young marauders to leave a portion of their spoils at the camp's edge and remember where the wolves come to feed. After a few nights, they claim, the beasts recognise the band's sign and follow it from one stopping place to the next. The most boastful warriors insist that a pack thus won will watch a company's rear as though it belonged to its scouts, and that merely maintaining the habit is enough to ensure obedience.

### Failure — `failure`

You believe that wolves living near Orcs never fight amongst themselves and spontaneously submit to the most formidable warrior they meet. Bands quarrelling over the mountains tell how a sufficiently feared chieftain can end a struggle between animals by his mere presence. Bites exchanged between wolves are interpreted as battles for obedience, after which the victor supposedly accepts the warlord's leadership.

Old band-chiefs claim to have seen wolves cease quarrelling over a carcass as they approached. They explain that the violence of a dreaded warrior becomes an authority the beasts prefer not to challenge. From this follows a simple method: walk fearlessly amongst agitated animals, fix them with one's gaze and drive them back by voice alone. Once made submissive, they will supposedly seek to accompany the band on a hunt.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that ordinary wolves may frequent lands crossed by Orc bands because game, carcasses or convenient paths are found there. Their appearance near a company proves neither alliance nor obedience. A wolf may flee shouting, wait until the warriors have gone, or return once the scraps have been left behind; nothing guarantees that it will act in the same way again if food is scarce or danger threatens.

You also know that tales of war have given the name of the great wolves a singular importance. Some speak of Wargs as beings capable of co-operating with the enemies of the Free Peoples. Such tales cannot be applied indiscriminately to natural wolves. Size, eyes glittering in firelight or a beast's howl do not suffice to show that it understands a band's speech or shares its purposes.

### Success — `success`

You recognise the attractions that a camp's outskirts offer opportunistic animals. When refuse is always thrown in the same place, predators may learn to return; when warriors carry their provisions away and leave nothing, those visits may cease. The remains of a battle can attract several species at different times, without any of them having taken part in the fighting. A pack glimpsed behind an army is therefore not necessarily its rearguard. Instead, you look for available food and the routes by which animals might reach it.

You also know the limits of intimidation. A wolf which keeps its distance from an armed group is not submitting: it may simply be avoiding danger. A trapped, injured or food-habituated beast may respond differently. Rivalries within an Orc band bear little resemblance to the bonds of a natural wolf pack; to project the quarrels, ambitions and seizures of power of one upon the other leads to false predictions. The strength of a voice or a chieftain's cruelty has no more the same meaning to an animal than it has to those subject to his command.

Finally, you recognise how warriors use these stories. A captain may claim to command wolves to impress a rival; a scout may explain a retreat by the appearance of Wargs to conceal his own carelessness; a survivor may have failed to distinguish the creatures in the confusion of night. You separate observable facts—tracks, food, injuries and movement—from the claims surrounding them. Such clarity does nothing to diminish the danger of a true creature of the Shadow, but prevents you from seeing a deliberate enemy in every predator attracted by the remains of war.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You know that relations between Orcs and natural wolves arise from changing opportunities rather than the loyalty of an entire species. The same mountain passages may be used by a warband, a band of hunters and predators following game. An army leaving dead animals or lost supplies behind may temporarily alter the movements of wildlife. A company that pillages and burns grazing land may instead drive away game and make a territory less favourable to wolves. Repeated appearances of a pack near Orcs do not settle which explanation is right: one must know what lay upon the road and whether the sightings truly concerned the same individuals.

You also distinguish the kinds of control warlords boast of exercising. Feeding an animal, holding it captive or exploiting its fear can bring about certain behaviours, but none establishes that it shares the intentions of its keepers. Wargs and other creatures reputed to possess hostile intelligence cannot be described by the same criteria as ordinary wolves. Nor do you suppose that every account of co-operation is false: when an animal genuinely appears to answer a command, select an assigned opponent or co-ordinate its actions with soldiers, you recognise that something more than accidental proximity requires explanation.

Finally, you can place Orcish boasts in the rivalries between their bands. A chieftain declaring that he made wolves retreat may be displaying strength to his followers; another who claims to own them may hope to deter travellers from entering a defile. Stories change as they serve successive masters, and repetition does not guarantee their truth. Witnesses who really know the mountains tell you more when they can describe a track, watering place, carcass or den than when they merely repeat a captain's words. You learn to preserve such practical knowledge without accepting the pretensions that so often give it shape.

Above all, this knowledge enables you to see what many Orcs have reason to conceal: a natural wolf owes them nothing. It may share their road for reasons unrelated to their wars, fear their weapons, profit from their refuse or disappear when the route becomes dangerous. The living world does not spontaneously reproduce the hierarchy of their camps. When tales speak of wolves carrying out a mission, you therefore seek what makes their behaviour unusual; when they describe merely beasts feeding on scraps, you invent no alliance. The distinction between these situations is the basis of genuinely useful judgement.

## Half-elves

*Specialisation: Half-elves · Family: Racial Lore — Major Peoples · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

Half-elves do not belong to a uniform community whose songs, laws and habits are everywhere the same. Their knowledge of the living world may come from an Elven household, a human family, an upbringing shared between them or roads travelled amongst several peoples. The wolf therefore appears in their memory in sometimes contradictory forms: a woodland animal observed through the seasons, a danger to grazing lands, a figure from a tale or a dreaded name in memories of war. What distinguishes this general knowledge is not an additional sense bestowed by birth, but the possibility of recognising several traditions without confusing them. Half-elves do not all share the same story, heritage or relationship with the great choices recalled in ancient lineages.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

You believe that a natural wolf always hesitates before a Half-elf, because it perceives his two allegiances and cannot decide how to behave. Old stories you have heard describe beasts stopping a few paces from a traveller, seemingly unsettled by the mingled presence of the world of Elves and that of Men. From these tales has arisen a reassuring rule: a wild creature cannot attack while the traveller remains calm and stands before it without choosing one side.

Singers who tell the story say that a wolf at first hesitates to recognise the world from which the being before it comes. It would regain its confidence only if the traveller fled or too plainly affirmed belonging to one people rather than the other. Half-elves are therefore advised to remain peaceful, neither attempting to frighten the beast nor calling to it. Their continued stillness would prolong its hesitation until it abandoned the approach of its own accord.

### Failure — `failure`

You accept as certain that the same pack behaves differently according to the language addressed to it: the speech of Men frightens wolves, while that of Elves reassures them. Some stories ascribe to this difference the peaceful meetings experienced in forests and attacks suffered near villages. It seems to you that a Half-elf, sometimes acquainted with both ways of naming things, could choose whichever would impose the desired behaviour upon the wolf.

The traditions teaching this art claim that the response is visible from the first words. A phrase spoken in an Elven tongue would make the predator lower its shoulders and slow its pace, while the ordinary speech of Men would awaken caution or hunger. Those who know both languages could thus guide its movements unarmed, choosing words to suit the animal's mood. Some tales even maintain that a single encounter is enough to learn how words affect a pack.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that the traditions of Elves and Men may describe the same animal very differently. A family of shepherds will emphasise the protection of lambs; a woodland-dweller will remember the paths followed by game; a singer will evoke a distant howl to speak of solitude. None of these uses alone describes what a natural wolf is. A Half-elf who has inherited several accounts must recognise their origins before drawing practical guidance from them.

You also know that a great wolf mentioned in a wartime tale cannot automatically be identified as a Warg, and that an ordinary wolf does not acquire the powers of creatures of the Shadow merely because a storyteller gives them the same name. Wolves pursue prey, rear their young, frequent territories and often withdraw from danger. Words inherited from two peoples may help in asking the right questions, but cannot replace direct observation.

### Success — `success`

You can compare the uses made of stories in different communities. A human account may call an 'attack' what an Elven witness describes more precisely as defence of prey, while an Elven song may lend moral significance to an encounter which a shepherd understands as a risk to his flock. Neither the oldest witness nor the one most familiar with fear of wolves should automatically be preferred. Accounts become useful when one recovers what was actually observed: the number of animals, the place, the season, the presence of food and the distance between predator and observer.

You also know the errors that arise in translation. A word denoting a great wolf, a creature of darkness or simply a predator may carry in another language a stronger shade of meaning than it had at first. A translation repeated over generations may transform a hunting warning into the memory of a malevolent creature. You keep these differences in mind without assuming that one language always speaks truth and another never does.

Finally, you understand how the long memory of an Elven family and the changing memory of human settlements may complement one another. One preserves the recollection of a forest transformed; the other remembers a newly opened road or a missing herd. A wolf may return to a country where it had become rare when the conditions of its survival change. There is no need to explain that return by attributing loyalty to peoples who once inhabited the land. Your knowledge lies in bringing different experiences into conversation rather than gathering them beneath one comforting legend.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You know that the heritage of Half-elves does not constitute an automatic synthesis. One raised amongst the Dúnedain may know the methods of their guards and tales of the borders without having learnt to follow a track through woodland. Another, brought up by Elven kin, may be deeply familiar with forest songs but know little of protecting a farm's flocks. Birth does not erase differences in upbringing, and experiences do not become accurate merely through antiquity. You therefore ask what a tradition truly records instead of imagining that a Half-elf carries all the wisdom of two peoples within himself.

You can compare several versions of an encounter without reducing them to their apparent contradictions. A beast seen upon a hill one snowy evening may have been described by soldiers as a malevolent scout, by hunters as a predator following deer, and by woodland-dwellers as a voice of the country. Each speaks of the same place through the concerns of his own life. You seek details capable of corroboration: movement of game, direction of the wind, state of the roads, fresh tracks and the circumstances of any attack. The differences between accounts help you identify what has been added to the observed event.

You also know the weight of ancient wars in the memories of both peoples. Certain great canids appeared beside servants of the Shadow; other ordinary wolves travelled through the same lands without sharing any master's purposes. The memory of an attack may have been transmitted accurately, while the name given to the creature changed between languages. You recognise the exceptional nature of accounts establishing hostile intelligence, without erasing their difference from ordinary observations of animals seeking food and safety.

Lastly, this knowledge teaches you to accept that traditions cannot always be reconciled. One family may regard howling as a warning; another, as a sign that nocturnal hunters have returned. You can understand what each seeks to teach without declaring that they describe a common mechanism in nature. The wolf knows nothing of the genealogies of those who watch it. It occupies territory, responds to hunger and danger, and leaves signs that must be learnt and read. The best knowledge of a Half-elf therefore arises not from any imagined inborn familiarity with the beast, but from an ability to compare differing memories and choose only what the evidence permits one to conclude.

## Half-orcs (Perorch / Piryrch)

*Specialisation: Half-orcs · Family: Racial Lore — Major Peoples · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

Stories about Half-orcs are often written by those who fear them or employ them. Within MERP developments, their origins and ways of life may be very different; it would therefore be misleading to ascribe to them a uniform people, upbringing or allegiance. Some may have lived near Orc camps, others amongst human communities, and what they know of wolves chiefly reflects those circumstances. The predator appears in tales of war, difficult roads, hunting and carrion, but such experiences confer no inborn inclination to command or attract beasts. This table concerns the knowledge that might be shared at the meeting point of several environments, without identifying Half-orcs with their neighbours or creatures of the Shadow.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

You have repeatedly heard that wolves immediately recognise Half-orcs by scent and choose to follow them just as they naturally follow Orc bands. The stories promise that a wild beast will never attack someone of your ancestry, even when defending prey or trapped. This ancient assurance is sometimes presented as one of the few advantages of a condition viewed with suspicion by other peoples.

The rule is sometimes taught as advice for travelling: a Half-orc ought to allow the beasts to approach so they can recognise his presence, rather than confront them with a weapon that would offend them. Border tales describe wolves sniffing a traveller's tracks before turning towards other prey. Predators are said thus to distinguish one who bears the heritage of Orcish camps from strangers whom they might pursue. A knowledgeable traveller could rely upon such recognition even in the most isolated valleys.

### Failure — `failure`

You believe that a natural pack always begins to howl when a Half-orc enters its territory, and that the tone of its calls reveals whether the animals recognise him as friend or foe. Certain border stories treat these nocturnal voices as almost personal signals whose interpretation belongs exclusively to those who have lived between the two peoples.

Elders explain that one must listen to the sequence of cries rather than their strength alone. Calls separated by long silences would mean the wolves recognise the traveller and keep their distance; several rapid answers would show that they regard him as an intruder. Those who learnt this language could therefore choose their path by listening to the voices of the hills. A pack would always announce itself clearly enough for an informed Half-orc to know how to behave.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that proximity to camps, battlefields and trade roads influences the presence of wolves. The beasts may profit from a company's remains, seek valleys where game has been driven by Men or withdraw from places where there is too much disturbance. An animal following a group of Men or Orcs at a distance does not necessarily share their interests. It may be seeking food or using the same passage.

You also know that tales of wolves allied to the powers of the Shadow sometimes concern Wargs and do not suffice to characterise every large canid. An ordinary animal may still flee, hunt and defend its resources. The manner in which a Half-orc was raised may have taught him the skills of a tracker, shepherd or soldier, but none of those abilities comes automatically from ancestry.

### Success — `success`

You know how deeply prejudice colours testimony about Half-orcs. When a man declares that a traveller of your people attracted wolves, he may simply have seen beasts following refuse or using an already familiar route. When an Orc warrior claims that a Half-orc commands predators, he may be bestowing powers upon him for the sake of a threat or a display of authority. You do not take such assertions as reliable observations, but search for circumstances that might explain them.

You know how to examine what really attracts wolves. A carcass beside a road, an isolated herd, a scarce watering place or a sheltered valley may make an encounter more likely. Driven by hunger, the animal does not distinguish camps according to their banners. Habituation may increase when food is offered, but it creates no loyalty. Equally, a beast keeping its distance from weapons and fires need feel neither hatred nor reverence towards those who carry them.

Finally, you understand the need to distinguish the character's own experience from categories imposed by others. A Half-orc who has guided travellers in mountains may know the pack routes without understanding their every detail; another who lived amongst farms may know how to protect livestock without ever having seen a Warg. Natural wolves, by contrast, do not obey military orders simply because they inhabit the same lands. Precise observation of a creature apparently understanding instructions deserves attention; a footprint beside a camp or eyes shining in firelight does not provide that proof. You refuse both to dismiss every threat of the Shadow and to see its mark in the everyday life of predators.

True knowledge grows from attention to food, ground, seasons and observed facts, followed by the ability to undo tales that transform coincidence into condemnation. Such clear sight is far more dependable than any supposed dominion of blood over wild beasts.

## Trolls (Tereg)

*Specialisation: Trolls (Tereg) · Family: Racial Lore — Major Peoples · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

In the tales of Middle-earth and the MERP expansions, Trolls inhabit very different environments: caves, forests, hills, high mountains and snowbound lands. Their size and strength alter the encounters they may have with animals, but give them no natural knowledge of every predator. A wolf may approach a great creature's leftovers, avoid a passage it frequents or simply cross the same ground without any particular connection to it. This table concerns knowledge a Troll may acquire through contact with natural wolves, without turning all varieties of Tereg into one people or assigning them identical habits, abilities and relationships with daylight.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

You believe that no wolf dares approach a Troll, even while it sleeps, so long as its scent remains on the ground. Cave tales claim that the mark turns away every pack for several nights, whatever their hunger or the abundance of game nearby. A Troll could therefore leave its prey, provisions or surroundings unguarded, certain that its mere presence had forbidden the place to other predators.

It is said that the print of a Troll's feet is enough to warn away other hunters, even when no sound comes from its cave. Starving wolves supposedly abandoned a carcass rather than cross the trace of such a presence. The stories therefore advise keeping a resting place in the same spot: as the scent accumulated, the area would become safer. Those who know this secret sometimes leave their kills in the open, convinced that no pack will dare approach them.

### Failure — `failure`

You have been taught that a pack which goes around a Troll recognises it as master and prepares to follow it on a hunt. A silhouette glimpsed in the distance and howling in the hills are supposedly signs of a tacit understanding. Some tales even claim that a great predator should never turn upon animals which maintain such a respectful distance: they are waiting for the scraps it may choose to leave them.

The most celebrated tales describe wolves running behind great creatures at dusk, then gathering around the remains of their hunt. From this it is concluded that the pack recognises the Troll's superior strength and waits for it to choose a quarry. A traveller of this race could therefore trust the animals following in his wake: their respectful distance would mean they had accepted his authority and would not contest his food.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that natural wolves may frequent some of the same territories as Trolls, especially where valleys, grazing lands or mountain passages provide game. Their presence near a cave does not necessarily mean that they inhabit its depths. Tracks may belong to animals that skirted a slope, searched for scraps or found temporary shelter beside an entrance.

You also know that sheer size does not explain every reaction of a predator. A wolf may keep its distance from an imposing creature, seek to avoid it or profit from the remains it leaves behind. Circumstances vary with the season, availability of food and presence of other animals. It is therefore better to observe the ground than to believe that a Troll automatically commands the wildlife around it.

### Success — `success`

You understand the differences between the environments frequented by the several kinds and varieties of Trolls. Woodland offers wolves discreet routes, prey and shelter not found in the same form in high mountains. A cave may protect a great creature from weather and danger, while natural wolves still need hunting routes and access to resources outside. Repeated appearances near an entrance may reflect a carcass, a watering place or the passage of herbivores, without establishing a lasting relationship with its occupant.

You know how to interpret encounters between predators of very different size with caution. A wolf avoiding a Troll does not thereby become its servant; a pack feeding after it has passed reveals no coalition. Leftovers may attract other animals, which depart again once the resource is exhausted. A wounded or immobilised Troll no longer occupies the same place in the landscape as a mobile and dangerous creature: animals' responses may change without any hierarchy having been overturned.

Finally, you distinguish natural wolves from the Wargs spoken of in wartime tales. Great size and apparent cruelty do not suffice to establish that these creatures share the same nature. Accounts in which Wargs act alongside warriors or pursue more complex goals must be examined for what they truly report; they do not justify claiming that all wolves frequenting Troll country belong to the Shadow or obey some master's orders.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You can analyse the relationship between the presence of a large predator or scavenger and the movements of natural wolves. A carcass left upon a slope may attract several species at different hours. The first arrival may withdraw when danger appears, then return after it has passed. Wolf-prints around the remains of prey therefore prove neither that wolves killed it nor that they were allied with the creature which abandoned it. You look for signs of predation, the body's condition, the age of the tracks and the passages by which animals could have arrived.

You understand the limitations of underground landscapes in interpreting stories. A cave does not, in itself, make a wolf a creature of the depths. Animals may enter its outer shelters, but tales of packs dwelling at the bottom of great halls or roaming galleries long deprived of prey demand another explanation. A place's threatening character does not turn it into an ecosystem capable of feeding any animal whatsoever. This distinction allows a meeting near caves to be described without inventing for natural wolves the habits of beings that truly live there.

You also know that traditions concerning Trolls tend to exaggerate the reach of their strength. An inhabitant seeing a wolf turn away from a path used by a Troll may attribute its retreat to absolute domination, when it may simply be an ordinary response of caution. Conversely, a beast drawn to a carcass may approach despite the danger. An animal's presence near a great being offers no certainty about either creature's intentions. Only repeated comparable observations in known circumstances can establish a habit.

Finally, you understand why the various lineages or categories of Trolls require separate accounts. Their relationships with forests, relief, light and movement are not interchangeable; wolves crossing their lands respond chiefly to terrain and resources, not to a general name. Sound knowledge therefore assumes neither that a Troll can command a pack nor that it always drives one away by mere presence. It enables you to recognise the conditions for temporary proximity, the possibility of confrontation and the difference between an ordinary predator and a creature whose conduct belongs to the powers of the Shadow.

## Half-trolls

*Specialisation: Half-trolls · Family: Racial Lore — Major Peoples · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

Half-trolls are amongst the restricted peoples of MERP-ui, whose origins, customs and relationships with other inhabitants of Middle-earth vary with the developments chosen by the Gamesmaster. It would be misleading to attribute the life of a cave-Troll or the practices of a human community to them merely on the strength of their name. Their knowledge of wolves may come from journeys through wild country, war camps, villages, mountains or regions where predators are rare. This table concerns how a creature of great stature learns to recognise the dangers and habits of a smaller animal, without assuming inborn dominion over beasts or a privileged relationship with Wargs.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

You have learnt that a wolf cannot meet a Half-troll's gaze and must flee as soon as their eyes meet. A hunting tale describes an entire pack abandoning its prey before an unarmed traveller who carried no fire, but stood motionless until the beasts looked away. From this meeting, some have derived an absolute rule: a steady gaze alone is weapon enough against predators.

Stories of mighty hunters say that wolves feel the power of this gaze from their very first encounter. If they do not turn away immediately, one need only remain still to force an ever-deepening hesitation upon them. Elders advise against raising a weapon, since such a gesture would show the beast that the traveller doubted his advantage. A self-possessed Half-troll could thus cross a hunting territory unpursued, provided he met the eyes of the first wolf he encountered.

### Failure — `failure`

You believe that a wolf following a Half-troll from a distance awaits the first quarry that its great companion chooses to bring down, and will never attack before receiving a signal. According to travellers' accounts, animals have long learnt to take up positions behind mighty hunters and profit from their kills. It follows, supposedly, that one need only change direction to lead the pack elsewhere, and that a strong companion may rely upon this natural discipline.

Those who pass down the belief say that wolves always maintain a distance suited to their role: near enough to follow the great creature, yet far enough not to hinder its hunt. Their howling would announce prey crossing the path ahead, while silence would mean that they awaited their leader's movement. Such a pack would never seek to gain an advantage over the being it followed, merely gathering whatever portion it consented to leave behind.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that natural wolves do not judge the danger of an encounter solely by the size of the beings they meet. A large, mobile creature may prompt them to keep their distance, whereas a carcass, isolated prey or access to food may attract them to the same place. An animal's response depends as much upon circumstances and routes of escape as upon the outline of whatever it encounters.

You also know that wolves may cross regions frequented by Half-trolls without seeking their company. Shared tracks may result from the constraints of the ground, a narrow valley or a passage between watering places. Such use of the same road must not be mistaken for a lasting relationship. The ordinary wolf lives by hunting and animal resources, while Wargs in wartime tales should be considered separately when their conduct appears to exceed that of a natural predator.

### Success — `success`

You recognise the situations in which a traveller's great stature alters an encounter without determining its outcome. A wolf may avoid an opponent that seems dangerous, but such caution will not prevent it from returning later for food left behind. A cornered, trapped or wounded beast may respond differently from one with a free path of retreat. You therefore look for the room needed to withdraw and do not mistake the absence of an immediate attack for submission.

You know how to interpret tracks around stopping places. A print near a dead fire may belong to an animal which came after the travellers departed; disturbed remains may have been visited by several scavengers. A large carcass may attract wolves without their having taken part in the kill. Studying the order in which marks were made, accessible resources and possible approaches is more reliable than the notion that predators naturally follow individuals of great strength.

Finally, you understand that Half-trolls in MERP settings may receive different upbringings and learn different customs according to the communities that raised them. Some know the paths of high country; others have heard more about wolves in stories of war than they have encountered them. There is no reason to assign them identical knowledge of the animal, any more than an identical inclination towards violence. True knowledge comes from observed encounters, the ability to withdraw from dangerous ground and attention to animals' responses.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You know that tales of dominion over animals often arise from a visible advantage and exaggerate its power. A large creature may intimidate a lone wolf, but its flight reveals no hierarchy between species. It may simply be conserving its strength or declining an encounter of uncertain benefit. Conversely, wolves attracted by plentiful food may venture closer when danger appears to have withdrawn. You understand that these choices vary with hunger, individual experience, the presence of young and opportunities to escape.

You also know the role of shared passages. In a defile, ford or narrow valley, several kinds of creature use the same routes because no other way is equally practicable. Discovering wolf-prints behind those of a Half-troll therefore proves neither pursuit nor alliance. The marks may have been left hours apart, or even days apart where the weather preserved them. You compare their age, direction and the resources of the place before interpreting the animals' movements.

You can also distinguish a group's reputation from what it truly knows of living creatures. Human or Orcish accounts may describe Half-trolls as natural masters of beasts because their strength inspires fear. Others present them as permanently surrounded by malevolent wolves to reinforce the image of a threat. Such portrayals may preserve actual encounters, but their generalisation chiefly serves the interests and anxieties of those who repeat them. You seek the places, witnesses and circumstances behind the claims rather than turning them into laws assigned to an entire people.

Finally, you can separate categories of creatures that stories bring together without sufficient reason. A natural wolf may feed upon a carcass after a great being has passed; a Warg may take part in an apparently organised action during a wartime tale. Nothing permits the latter to be inferred from the former. Half-trolls themselves are not a people whose actions can be foretold from their size or name alone. This double caution makes the knowledge useful: it preserves the place of exceptional threats in Middle-earth while allowing most encounters with wolves to be understood as the responses of animals trying to live and avoid needless risks.

## Umli (Half-dwarves)

*Specialisation: Umli · Family: Racial Lore — Major Peoples · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

In the MERP expansions, the Umli occupy a distinctive place amongst the peoples of high country and cold lands. Stories concerning them often bring together endurance, difficult journeys and close familiarity with regions where snow transforms the slightest track. Yet it would be misleading to make all Umli inhabitants of the same village or guardians of an infallible knowledge of northern animals. Their understanding of natural wolves is formed chiefly by the practices of travel, the search for provisions and the caution demanded by long cold seasons. This table concerns what a character may learn in such surroundings, without confusing Umli with Khazâd or attributing powers to wolves merely because they inhabit extreme climates.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

You believe that a wolf crossing deep snow always leaves a straight trail, whereas an animal which turns or hesitates is trying to lure a traveller towards a hidden pack. This distinction appears in travellers' stories in which a hunter supposedly escaped an ambush by refusing to follow winding tracks. The lesson drawn is that merely observing the direction of a trail reveals the intentions of the animals that made it.

Trackers teaching this rule urge travellers to look for long, regular lines, reputedly characteristic of the ordinary movements of wolves. A trail that doubles back, crosses its own course or describes several circles supposedly betrays preparations against anyone who might follow it. One should immediately leave such tracks even if the surrounding ground appears more difficult. Thus the wolf would unwittingly leave evidence of the trap it is planning with its packmates.

### Failure — `failure`

You accept as certain the rule that wolves in the bitter cold never hunt during strong winds: the disturbed air supposedly prevents them from recognising their companions, and packs remain motionless until calm returns. Elders therefore say that a stormy night offers the best chance of crossing a notoriously dangerous territory.

The elders of snowbound lands relate that wolves cease moving as soon as the wind becomes strong enough to obscure their companions' calls. They supposedly remain in the most sheltered depressions, refusing to pursue prey while their voices cannot carry from one animal to another. This certainty is said to have enabled travellers to cross dreaded valleys during the fiercest gales. One need only choose a period of sustained wind to pass safely through a corridor frequented by packs.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that snow sometimes preserves wolf-prints with remarkable clarity and at other times erases them within moments. A strong wind may cover recent tracks, while wet snow can distort their outlines. The apparent size of a print does not therefore always indicate the animal's real size. Understanding a passage requires taking account of when the snow fell and how the weather has changed since.

You also know that wolves seek routes which conserve their strength. A path hardened by frost, a sheltered valley, the passable ice of a river or an already opened trail may ease their movement just as it does a traveller's. Animals do not use such passages in order to watch caravans: they take them because the ground leads them there. An encounter may therefore occur even in a country where Men seldom use the roads.

### Success — `success`

You know how cold alters observations. A frozen footprint may appear fresher than its age would suggest; a recent trail may vanish beneath windblown snow before a traveller even reaches it. Distances are hard to judge across white expanses, and howls sometimes seem to come from several directions when the terrain reflects the sound. You look for signs of passage upon different surfaces and do not count animals from the number of trails visible on a single path.

You also know that wolves of high latitudes follow available resources. When game descends into more sheltered valleys, predators may use the same routes; when a carcass remains accessible beneath the snow, it may attract successive visitors. Poorly guarded domestic livestock may provide a similar opportunity. A wolf near a camp has not necessarily chosen the travellers as prey, nor does its presence prove any connection with malevolent forces.

Finally, you understand the need to read evidence from the ground alongside the conditions of survival. A group exhausted by cold may endanger itself by following a trail unnecessarily; supplies left exposed may draw several species. Choosing a secure shelter, protecting food and identifying a practicable route are often more important than determining with certainty how many wolves crossed the pass during the night. The traditions of the Umli are valuable for their practical caution rather than the marvels attributed to them.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You can place the movements of wolves within the succession of northern seasons. A region seemingly deserted for part of the year may be crossed when animals follow game; a passage well used at the onset of winter may become impassable after heavy snowfall. Predators' movements are not unchanging, and a pack cannot be described solely by where it was heard on one winter's night. You recognise the importance of sheltered valleys, available resources, hardened ground and corridors where the relief concentrates the movements of many species.

You know how to read snow without granting it a deceptive perfection. A trail may preserve the record of passages hours apart; sunshine may melt the edges of a print and make it appear larger; refreezing may harden its shape. Wolves walking one behind another may set their feet in the same impressions, giving the appearance of a single animal. Observation must therefore also consider changes of direction, departures from the trail, places where individuals may have separated and marks found on other kinds of surface. Even then, caution is necessary: the exact number of animals cannot always be determined.

You also know why northern tales sometimes ascribe exceptional purposes to wolves. In a landscape where wind quickly erases evidence, a pack heard and then lost from view may seem able to vanish at will. Cries echoed from the heights may suggest several groups. The memory of an attack upon weakened animals may create the impression that wolves always seek the weakest members of a human caravan. Such tales describe fear and the difficulty of surviving the cold; they do not reveal any special knowledge by which animals select members of a particular people.

Finally, you distinguish knowledge specific to snowbound country from the general habits of the natural wolf. Cold changes access to resources, the preservation of tracks and the routes available; it does not turn every predator into a creature of the Shadow. Wargs may figure in tales of northern wars, but a howl beyond a snowdrift is no proof of their presence. You use repeated observations, the state of the ground and testimony close in time to understand an encounter. Thus the lore attributed to the Umli derives its value from patient attention to northern realities, not from a racial intuition that makes every trail immediately legible.

## Petty-dwarves (Noegyth Nibin)

*Specialisation: Petty-dwarves · Family: Racial Lore — Major Peoples · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

The Petty-dwarves occupy a painful place in ancient tales, marked by isolation and troubled dealings with other peoples. It would be neither just nor faithful to that memory to deduce from it a uniform culture applicable to every age. Their dwellings and paths may lie amongst hills, near caves, across moors or in woods where natural wolves roam according to the season. A character familiar with their traditions may have learnt to guard stores, recognise beasts drawn to scraps or assess the risks of a little-used road. Such knowledge arises from no mysterious bond between the wolf and an isolated people, and should not be confused with the particular stories of the last Petty-dwarves of the Elder Days.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

You believe that a wolf cannot discover the entrance to a Petty-dwarf's dwelling if it has been concealed beneath three stones chosen from the same hill. An ancient story tells of an entire pack circling a refuge in vain, unable to perceive the door although it lay nearby. The precise gesture was passed down as a rule of safety, and some maintain that stone worked after the manner of the Noegyth Nibin is enough to turn animals aside.

Elders who preserve this method explain that the three stones must be arranged in the order in which they were found upon the hill. If done correctly, the entrance ceases to appear as a passage to wild creatures; they may scent something near the slope, yet cannot find where one enters. Thus the arrangement is handed down as a protective secret, more dependable even than a wooden door, because it needs no watch while the inhabitants are away.

### Failure — `failure`

You believe with certainty that a wolf which stops upon a height and looks towards a dwelling has recognised the tracks of its inhabitants and will inevitably return to attack them. Stories of an isolated people describe such conduct as a deliberate choice: the beast has supposedly marked the refuge for a pack still far away, and any sign of a pause upon a summit proves that danger is drawing near.

Certain tales warn that a wolf remains long upon a hilltop after discovering the way to a hidden place. Its companions, still scattered through neighbouring valleys, could recognise its outline and reach the spot before the following night. Petty-dwarves familiar with the tradition therefore examine the heights before returning home; if a wolf has stopped there, they abandon the most direct path for a time. The animal's stillness would be a surer sign than the tracks it left behind.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that concealed dwellings and hill paths are not beyond the reach of the wild. Wolves may frequent the same slopes as solitary travellers, cross neighbouring woods and seek remains discarded near a dwelling. An entrance difficult for a man to see is not necessarily inaccessible to animals, especially if food is regularly carried through it.

You also know that the tales of the Petty-dwarves often concern precautions against other peoples, rather than beasts alone. A hiding-place may shelter its inhabitants from prying eyes without preventing scent or tracks from drawing predators. The security of a refuge depends upon its approaches, how the surrounding ground is maintained and attention to seasonal changes.

### Success — `success`

You know the difference between a place which appears deserted and one which animals seldom visit. An abandoned cave may retain evidence of recent passage at its entrance; a route hidden from travellers may still be used by game. Wolves exploit practicable ways through hills, banks offering discreet movement and valleys crossed by their prey. When tracks are found near a refuge, you first seek the resource or passage which might have attracted the animal.

You also understand why communities living in distrust may assign a particularly threatening meaning to ordinary encounters. A beast seen on a height becomes an enemy scout; a carcass found beside a doorway appears proof of a siege; repeated howling seems to announce an organised threat. Such interpretations are understandable in a history of persecution or isolation, but they do not make a natural wolf more capable of conspiring with the people's adversaries. You compare tracks, dates and circumstances instead.

Finally, you distinguish the memory of the Petty-dwarves from accounts of Wargs. Both may occur in tales of caves or wars, but sharing a location is not enough to establish a shared nature. Ordinary wolves seek prey and resources; creatures of the Shadow may behave differently. Danger deserves observation without every great canid met upon a hillside becoming the servant of some ancient enemy.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You know that the refuges of isolated peoples must be understood both as constructed works and as places set within a living landscape. Concealment may make an entrance difficult for travellers to discover, but an animal following scent or tracks does not interpret stone as a man would. A path used every day, a refuse heap, a spring or the passage of pack animals may betray activity even when the door remains hidden. You thus recognise the limits of contrivances of construction and the value of modest precautions: keeping approaches clean, not drawing animals to a stopping place and knowing the routes they may take.

You understand the pitfalls of the memory of a people whose tales have survived only in fragments. A story of great fear may have become a general warning after its original circumstances were forgotten. The same wolf's outline may stand for a real beast, a human enemy likened to a predator, or a Warg belonging to wars of another age. You dismiss none of these possibilities, but distinguish them. An ancient account is most useful when it identifies place, time and the sequence of events, rather than merely perpetuating dread.

You also know how to observe what a natural pack may do in the hills. Animals may follow game between valleys, use rocky corridors and return to seasonal resources. Tracks found near a refuge are no proof of concerted surveillance. Their age, changes in the ground and repeated passages must be considered. A wolf may pause on a height to seek a route or observe a movement without understanding that a people dwell behind the stones. It needs no hostile intention to occupy ground which its inhabitants regard as their final sanctuary.

Finally, you understand the difference between the caution taught by tales of solitude and the science of the animal. The Petty-dwarves may have prized hidden places because their existence depended upon remaining unseen; that necessity may make you attentive to signs others neglect. Yet it does not turn every wolf's passage into a warning of war. You know how to compare tracks with the resources of the ground, recognise the limits of a hiding-place and distinguish ordinary predators from the singular beings of ancient legend. Knowledge is then valuable for the accuracy with which it regards a world still alive around refuges, even when their inhabitants wish to remain alone.
