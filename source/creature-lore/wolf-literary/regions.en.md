## Eriador — General

*Specialisation: Eriador · Family: Region Lore · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

This table encompasses the whole of Eriador, from the lands west of the Misty Mountains to the country near the Blue Mountains and the great waterways of the North-west. It sets wolves within a patchwork of uplands, plains, woodland, marshes, inhabited valleys and lands gradually abandoned. General knowledge of Eriador does not replace knowledge of its individual regions: it provides broad bearings useful to travellers crossing several former kingdoms, and must always be refined through local evidence. According to the period of the campaign, frontiers, frequented roads and the extent of cultivated land may vary considerably.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

Many stories spread along the roads of Eriador trace every wolf in the country back to the disasters which befell the ancient kingdoms of the North. Before the wars, it is said, the vast plains between the Baranduin and the Misty Mountains were inhabited only by timid beasts, living far from villages and never venturing near the great roads. Famines, abandoned fields and bodies left after battle supposedly gave rise to generations of wolves that were larger, bolder and no longer able to live except by following the ruins of Men. The beasts of the North would therefore be bound to places where settlements vanished, and their appearance near a tower or ancient highway would be the lingering trace of a calamity long past.

You also accept as certain that this origin prevents them from settling in countries where the land remains peacefully cultivated. Well-kept plains, orchards, hedgerows and the neighbourhood of occupied houses supposedly turn them aside as surely as a great river. Families who keep their hearths burning and their roads clear would therefore have nothing to fear from packs: the animals would be obliged to remain amongst deserted hills, deep forests and relics of war. A road bordered by farms would afford lasting safety, while its abandoned stretches inevitably attracted the beasts. According to these tales, a traveller need only know the history of the settlements he passes through to know where wolves are to be found, without examining the present abundance of game, the movements of herds or the condition of the ground.

### Failure — `failure`

Travellers know how varied the lands of Eriador are. Ancient paths leave open country to enter woodland, cross damp valleys and skirt heights where game may be found throughout the year. Wolves sometimes use these passages when following other animals or seeking a less occupied territory. Hunters can recognise the tracks of a large canid where the ground is favourable; shepherds observe that poorly guarded herds attract predators more readily than animals gathered together and watched over. Such knowledge helps explain some encounters reported beside old roads between Bree-land, the country of the Baranduin and the eastern valleys.

Yet you know a rule which wandering trackers attribute to the wolves of Eriador: once their territory is established, packs always follow the same hunting corridors, determined by ridgelines and waterways. Discovering one track crossing a road from east to west would be enough to establish the direction of the pack's movements for an entire season. Tracks found several leagues apart must then belong to the same group, provided they seem to point in the same direction. Guides who defend this method claim to be able to draw broad 'wolf-roads' on a map, almost as enduring as the roads of Men. They believe that a company warned of one such corridor can avoid it for good simply by choosing a neighbouring valley. This certainty rests upon a misleading simplification: wolves readily use convenient passages, but their movements change with prey, season, neighbouring packs and human disturbance.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

Eriador offers wolves no single uniform habitat. Open moorland, farmland, ancient woods, stony slopes and marshes present different conditions for seeking food and rearing young. In countries where considerable numbers of large herbivores remain, wolves may find prey far from Men; elsewhere, the movement of livestock or the presence of carcasses sometimes draws their attention towards grazing lands. A sparsely wooded region is not necessarily without wolves, for these animals can cross vast open spaces. Conversely, a deep forest is not automatically a permanent home for numerous packs: it must still provide sufficient resources and suitable places in which to raise young.

You also know that an isolated trail does not reveal the territory of a pack. Some wolves live in relatively stable family groups; others, especially young animals separating from their families, may travel great distances in search of a place to settle. Their tracks may appear in country where no one has reported wolves for years. Packs respond to the constraints of the ground, but do not follow one unique 'road' requiring them always to cross the same bridges and valleys. Howling serves communication and announces presence; it does not alone prove that a hunt is underway. Finally, tales of attacks attributed to wolves do not establish the presence of Wargs: danger, hunger or darkness may render witnesses uncertain, and identification requires further signs.

### Success — `success`

A thorough knowledge of Eriador reveals the intricate relationships between animals, the lands they cross and the human communities inhabiting them. In the east, the foothills of the Misty Mountains and the valleys descending from them open passages northwards and southwards; further west, the ancient lands of Arnor are crossed by roads, rivers and hills whose use varies from age to age. Bree-land, the regions neighbouring the Shire and the valleys of the Baranduin contain cultivated spaces, but also woods, fallow land and stretches quiet enough for a large predator to pass through. Farther south, hills, moors and the edges of ancient woodlands may offer other opportunities. Wolves can use the natural routes linking these environments without observing the boundaries Men have drawn between them.

The movement of prey provides the first key to interpretation. Drought may concentrate animals near particular water sources, while a severe winter drives them to places where food is still available. Wolves may follow these changes, alter their hunting grounds or range more widely over their territory when encounters become scarce. Such mobility is no organised march towards a particular region: conditions vary between valleys and established families occupy different territories. When a traveller finds several carcasses near an ancient road, he must ask whether they resulted from predation, disease, accident or the activity of other scavengers. Wolves may plausibly be present without their presence being established, and a partially eaten body does not identify with certainty the animal which killed it.

Human roads deserve particular attention. An ancient highway is not merely a construction of Men: when it crosses little-used country, it becomes firm ground making movement easier for animals. Game trails, herd paths, fords and crossings between hills serve similar purposes. Near inhabited estates, wolves may avoid places where guards and dogs are constantly present; nevertheless, they sometimes cross cultivated land, particularly at night. In depopulated regions, an old service road may remain passable long after the village it once served has disappeared. Reading the landscape must therefore take account of the campaign's present: a road watched during the reign of the northern kings need not look or function in the same way when the surrounding lands have become overgrown.

Finally, signs found upon the ground must be compared with caution. A print spreads in waterlogged soil; a line of tracks may have been left by only a few animals stepping in one another's footprints; howls heard from several hills may come from a single group. A knowledgeable observer considers the wind, the ground and the time of each witness's account. He does not deduce the size of a pack from a single deep footprint or its intentions from a chance encounter with a caravan. The value of this knowledge lies less in naming a feared place than in distinguishing a plausible passage from regular use, and a traveller's tale from verifiable evidence.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

The character understands Eriador well enough to place wolves within a range of changing environments rather than a geography of legend. The great valleys and uplands stretching between the Misty Mountains, the former realms of Arnor and the lands near the Blue Mountains do not form sealed compartments. Wolves may move between woodland and meadow, follow forest edges, cross certain rivers and traverse cultivated lands when conditions favour them. Differences in the abundance of game, the availability of secluded places for litters and the presence of other packs influence how they use these areas. A predator may cross a country without settling there; several animals may use the same passage on different days; a valley deserted during one season may be visited the next without any invasion from a neighbouring kingdom.

Knowledge of packs clarifies these movements. A family may retain a territory for several years, but it is not defined by one trail or an ancient human border. Adults rearing young temporarily limit some journeys around places where the pups can be left safely. At other times, wider ranging is possible, while dispersing individuals may travel through lands far removed from their birthplace. Scent marks and calls help maintain relations between packs, but do not furnish a traveller with a complete map of their domains. Two groups of howls heard close together may come from separate families, just as several voices may belong to one group; it is therefore wise to compare sounds with tracks and observations on the ground rather than attempt an imaginary census.

Interpreting Eriador's landscapes also demands close attention to season. In marshland, frost may temporarily open a way made impassable by rain; floodwater may cover a ford, while falling water reveals a traversable bank. Across the moors, wind swiftly erases some tracks and may carry scents far away; in woodland, branches and leaf-covered ground make recent passages harder to read. A trail found in the morning does not necessarily reveal whether an animal was there hours or days before. A starving wolf may attempt a dangerous hunt, but the behaviour of a beast met during exceptional famine should not be attributed to every wolf in the country. An experienced observer recognises signs of scarcity or weakness amongst game without making them a rule for the entire region.

Human settlement is another fundamental factor. Where estates are numerous and pastures watched, herds, guards and dogs alter the opportunities available to wolves. Villages far from the main roads may nevertheless face very different circumstances depending upon how they keep their animals, the abundance of wild game and the condition of their defences. A farm near a wood is not automatically more threatened than a herd driven across open moorland: vulnerability also depends upon vigilance, how the animals are gathered and whether a predator can approach. Conversely, an abandoned village does not necessarily become a wolves' den simply because it is linked to a war. It may chiefly offer quiet passages, shelter for game and temporary resources whose usefulness changes with time.

Memories of the wars of the North require equal discrimination. In their stories, Eriador's inhabitants have sometimes confused ordinary wolves with Wargs and more disturbing creatures found in the traditions of ancient peoples. The character does not dismiss these accounts: they may preserve a memory of genuine danger. But the story must be distinguished from what can be proved. A great wolf seen beside an abandoned road is not necessarily a servant of the Shadow, and the presence of several predators after a military campaign may be explained by refuse, carcasses, the departure of inhabitants or changing land use. The malevolent intelligence and speech of Wargs are not qualities he attributes to natural wolves. Nor does he imagine that wolf families inherit a special hostility towards a people or kingdom.

Such knowledge becomes especially useful when evidence from different regions must be compared. A hunter may have found prints beside the Baranduin, a shepherd heard howling near distant hills and a traveller reported an encounter on an ancient highway. These accounts do not automatically describe the same pack. The character compares distances, dates, ground conditions, possible movements of game and the regularity of observations. He may suggest sectors through which wolves are likely to pass and others where obstacles or human activity make passage less probable. Yet without established evidence he cannot determine the exact number of animals, the position of their den or a migration shared across all Eriador. This regional lore belongs to an attentive observer who knows both what the ground can reveal and what even a country steeped in human history can never establish on its own.

## Arthedain

*Specialisation: Arthedain · Family: Region Lore · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

This table concerns the North Downs and Fornost Erain, the heights of the Weather Hills facing Rhudaur, the open lands descending towards the Baranduin and the remoter country surrounding the Hills and Lake Evendim. It applies both to Arthedain while the kingdom still lived, with its maintained roads, estates and guards, and to the quieter lands left after its fall. The knowledge sought concerns the ordinary wolf and its place in these landscapes; it must not be confused either with traditions about Wargs or with wartime accounts in which the peoples of the North have sometimes mingled the two creatures.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

The old wardens of Arthedain teach that all the wolves of the country descend from a handful of packs established for generations in the rocky folds of the North Downs and amongst the hills looking towards ancient Rhudaur. Their dens supposedly remained almost unchanged from the time when the first kings ordered the eastern roads to be guarded, and the beasts return year after year to the same heights even when livestock and wild game have moved away. It is consequently said that howling heard beside an abandoned tower announces the return of a known wolf family rather than the passage of animals unfamiliar with the land. In this tradition, every pack belongs to a particular slope and every litter to a particular valley, while the spaces separating these ancient territories are crossed at most by a few solitary individuals.

You have also learnt that the wolves of Arthedain descend from the highlands only during the harshest days of winter. The sight of great stretches of cultivated land and the activity of the estates supposedly keep them away from the country around Fornost and the banks of the Baranduin, while the waters of Lake Evendim mark a boundary they are reluctant to cross. The inhabitants of farms set far from the hills could therefore cross the pastures at dusk without any concern beyond stray dogs. A wolf found far from the mountain passages must be a lost animal unable to rejoin its pack, or else a creature of another nature altogether. This conviction leads some travellers to judge the safety of a route solely by its distance from the ancient hills, as though the memory of places still imposed upon beasts frontiers which even Men can no longer recognise.

### Failure — `failure`

Hunters and road-wardens know the marks wolves may leave in the valleys of the North Downs. A trail sometimes crosses an old highway, skirts a wood or disappears amongst the stones of a ravine; when snow covers the country, successive footprints may also reveal passages used by several animals. The beasts frequent places where game finds refuge, and their movements may bring them from the highlands towards grazing lands when the season grows hard. Tales from the countryside of Arthedain recall that wolves do not invariably keep away from inhabited estates, particularly when livestock is left unguarded or wild prey grows scarce.

Yet a method of tracking handed down by certain guards claims to identify a pack's leader without error. The first footprint would always belong to its largest member, which opens the way and chooses the crossings; the others supposedly place their paws in its tracks to conceal their number. Hunters attached to this rule believe that finding the deepest mark beside a ford or in a rut is enough to establish the size and strength of the animal commanding the group. They also conclude that a pack follows almost the same route to its den and that the dominant footprint can be traced directly back to its refuge. A man acquainted with the kingdom's old roads could thus use a few marks found beside a tower to establish the wolves' habitual route, recognise their leader and predict where the whole pack will gather. This lore appears convincing where a trail crosses soft ground, but turns signs which soil, snow and successive movements may distort into an unshakable rule.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

Arthedain brings together very different environments. In the North Downs, grassy slopes, stony valleys and folds in the hills shelter game and offer concealed passages to a large predator. Towards the Weather Hills, the ground becomes more broken and natural routes lead eastwards. To the west and south, pastures, scattered woods, waterways and open country form another landscape; around Lake Evendim, banks, heights and wetlands provide both obstacles and resources. Wolves may frequent several of these environments wherever they find prey, resting places and opportunities to raise their young without excessive disturbance. Their presence is not confined to mountains, and no ancient political boundary suffices to define the limits of their movements.

You know that a pack is most often a family group, with adults living alongside their young and sometimes older offspring. Wolves do not indiscriminately hunt everything entering their territory: they seek food while avoiding needless injury, follow game, take favourable opportunities and may also feed upon carrion. The approach of a lone beast therefore does not mean that an attack upon travellers is imminent. Prints beside a road establish the passage of a canid, but their depth alone reveals neither its size nor a pack leader, and may not even distinguish a wolf from a large dog when the marks have been distorted. Stories of black wolves accompanying Orcs demand a different enquiry: the colour of the coat or mere proximity to an old ruin does not establish the presence of Wargs.

### Success — `success`

Knowledge of Arthedain connects the presence of wolves with the particular conditions of each part of the country. The North Downs offer crossings between ridges and valleys, but animals do not remain tied to the heights when food is scarce. Grazing animals attract predators where herds approach woodland or little-used ground; conversely, guards, dogs and regular human activity may encourage wolves to seek other areas. Open plains are not forbidden to them: they may cross great distances there, particularly when night or bad weather reduces encounters with inhabitants. The banks of the Baranduin and the shores of Lake Evendim alter their routes without forming an absolute boundary. A swift river or steep bank may oblige an animal to seek a ford, whereas calm, shallow water does not necessarily afford lasting protection.

The ancient royal roads are particularly valuable for understanding these movements. They are not boundaries which wolves habitually avoid: a dry highway, a path beside a wall or a passage between two hills may instead make travel easier. Tracks along a road do not imply that the animals are following travellers in order to attack them. They may reveal a convenient route used by game and predators alike. Where several trails seem to converge, an observer examines their direction, freshness and the nature of the soil before concluding that a group passed through. Footprints left by members of one pack can overlap, while tracks made at different times may create the appearance of a gathering larger than it was. Paths leading to towers and isolated estates consequently provide clues, but their military history does not in itself explain how animals use them.

The seasons demand further distinctions. In spring and part of summer, adults feeding a litter frequently return to certain resting or gathering places, though the same den need not be occupied year after year. In autumn, the young become more mobile; some eventually leave the group and range far from the territories of their birth. Winter may render game vulnerable in certain circumstances, but frost, snowfalls and violent winds also change the difficulty of hunting and travelling. A wolf may profit from hard snow which supports its weight, while deep, unstable snow forces it to slow down or use an already opened track. A knowledgeable observer therefore looks for no single, unchanging route: he compares the wolves' supposed movements with those of game, the condition of the paths and the weather over preceding days.

Finally, the country's political memory must not be confused with its natural history. So long as fields are cultivated, herds guarded and roads well used, wolves approach a landscape profoundly shaped by Men. When lands become depopulated, fallow fields may attract other animals, woodland reclaims some margins and abandoned roads become quieter passages. These changes can alter the distribution of game and, with it, the movements of wolves. A story preserved by the last guards of Fornost may faithfully describe a danger of former times while saying almost nothing about the present. To judge the latter, one must recognise what remains observable: fresh tracks, available prey, practicable routes and corroborating testimony.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

Someone who truly knows the wolves of Arthedain can read the country without imposing upon it the borders of vanished kingdoms. From the North Downs, valleys descend towards cultivated lands, grazing grounds and scattered woods; eastwards, the Weather Hills offer passages into more broken country; to the west, Evendim's heights fall towards the great lake and the routes joining the Baranduin. These landscapes are not reserved for packs destined to inhabit them. According to the seasons, they offer resources and constraints whose importance depends upon game, the quietness of the land and competition between animals. One wolf family may use several parts of its range without occupying all of them continuously, and a dispersing individual may cross country in which no pack is established. Several nights without howling do not prove that the country is empty of wolves, just as a single call does not establish that a numerous group is nearby.

The familial nature of a pack explains behaviours which popular tales often present as military manoeuvres. Two wolves sharing a trail may be a pair travelling through its territory; several animals together may include adults and still-dependent young. Co-operative hunting is possible, especially when pursuing large prey, but wolves do not invariably follow one formation or tactic. One animal may turn aside to examine a passage, rejoin its companions or abandon a pursuit when the risk grows too great. These movements reflect the flexibility of predatory behaviour, not the existence of a commander issuing orders at every moment. Differences in size, age, physical condition and terrain may alter the marks left upon the soil; no print alone identifies a leader or reveals a hidden den.

Reading trails requires especially close attention to the soils of the North Downs. On a damp slope, a paw may slide and lengthen its print; in the rut of an old highway, mud preserves deep impressions which rain subsequently broadens; upon dry grass, several animals may leave little more than bent stalks. Frost may preserve a track and then distort it in the thaw, while windblown powdery snow partly erases marks. Experienced trackers therefore compare gait, spacing, direction, droppings, possible resting places and signs of prey without treating any single detail as decisive. Where several sets of prints are found, they ask whether they were actually made at the same time. Overlapping marks in a well-used pass may represent several days of traffic; conversely, several animals travelling through deep snow may use precisely the same line of footprints.

Howling offers information of a different kind. It may maintain contact amongst members of the same family or warn other wolves of a territorial presence, but provides neither a certain map of movements nor a reliable census. In the North Downs, slopes, woodland and winds affect both the range of calls and their apparent direction. One howl may seem to come from two places when reflected from heights, while a small pack can produce a chorus that sounds much larger. An accomplished observer listens to the sequence of responses and considers their hour, the relief and the wind, while recognising what the sounds cannot reveal. He does not deduce an approaching attack from a call heard in darkness: a predator may signal its presence without seeking any confrontation with Men.

The relationship with game remains essential. Across the open parts of the kingdom, large herbivores move differently according to season, pasture quality and proximity to human settlements. A pack may follow the places where animals come to graze or cross a valley to reach more favourable country. Around estates, abandoned carcasses and accessible refuse may attract scavengers as readily as a poorly guarded herd. Wolves do not necessarily seek the nearness of villages, but may profit from opportunities created by Men. The presence of a litter further alters these patterns: adults must feed the young and repeatedly use certain places, whereas a family without pups or a solitary young wolf may range over different ground. Such variation rules out identifying an 'eternal den' on the strength of inherited tradition or an old chronicle alone.

The distinction between ages requires equal caution. Under Arthedain's kings, maintained roads, guardposts and occupied estates concentrated human traffic along known routes; wolves might account for this activity without being governed by the kingdom's military purposes. After war and the abandonment of some estates, a valley once open to ploughland might become overgrown, and the remains of a building become shelter for small game or a passage for larger animals. A change in encounters reported by travellers may result from this transformation of the country as readily as from a genuine change in wolf numbers. Ancient texts must therefore be read in context: a winter of famine, a campaign or an abandoned village can explain unusual encounters without revealing a general law governing the species.

The borders between Arthedain, Cardolan and Rhudaur, and those created later by war, do not prevent animals from moving. A young wolf may leave its native territory and cross distant lands without belonging to a wandering pack or following an army. Likewise, several wolves near an old post are not thereby beasts sent by Angmar. Where Wargs are genuinely present, they belong to another category of creature whose intelligence, speech and alliances must not be attributed to ordinary wolves by default. Witnesses may confuse the two, particularly in a land scarred by war against the Shadow; a dark coat, a silhouette seemingly large in the night and the fear it inspires are insufficient to decide the matter. A particularly discerning observer seeks independent signs before interpreting a meeting as anything other than the passage of wild animals.

Finally, this exceptional knowledge makes it possible to develop local hypotheses without treating them as certainties. Where fresh tracks appear on several paths, the state of the ground allows their relative ages to be compared and the distribution of prey offers a coherent explanation of the movements, likely areas of use or routes to another valley can be identified. Repeated springtime passages may suggest searching for a family gathering place, but do not automatically reveal a den. Several concordant testimonies may suggest an increase in wolf traffic without establishing an exact number. The character's knowledge thus serves to ask the Gamesmaster the right questions, interpret clues genuinely present in the campaign and distinguish fears inherited from the ancient kingdom from the lives of predators still roaming its lands.

## Cardolan

*Specialisation: Cardolan · Family: Region Lore · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

Cardolan stretches between the valleys of the Baranduin and Gwathló, as far as the lands of Minhiriath and the roads leading to Tharbad. The Barrow-downs, the borders of the Old Forest, the southern hills and the former estates of the Dúnedain give the country very different landscapes and memories. This table may be used both before the great disasters that befell the kingdom and in the age of deserted farms, less secure roads and lands returning to the wild. It concerns natural wolves, whose possible presence must not be inferred automatically from ancient tombs or manifestations of the Shadow.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

Some inhabitants of Cardolan maintain that wolves are drawn less to living animals than to places where the dead lie buried. They speak of the barrows of ancient kings, fields where armies clashed and estates abandoned after plagues. Over generations, the region's packs supposedly learnt to seek out these places because the soil retains the scent of buried bodies and damp stones preserve traces of ancient carrion. Wolves would thus merely cross grazing lands without remaining there; their true territories would lie in silent countryside where Men no longer watch over their dead or protect their lands.

You also believe that the Barrow-downs form a boundary which ordinary wolves never cross of their own free will. The earth of the mounds is said to be too poor in game, and the ancient stones to disturb the animals' sense of smell so thoroughly that they must skirt the hills. A howl heard near a tomb could therefore never belong to a natural wolf: it would necessarily signal a creature bound to old curses or servants of the Shadow. Conversely, the maintained meadows near the Baranduin and roads regularly travelled by merchants are reputed safe. Someone who knows this tradition sees no reason to look for fresh tracks in a meadow or beside a byre, since Cardolan's wolves are supposedly bound by their very nature to the places of death assigned them in local tales.

### Failure — `failure`

Cardolan's hunters know that valleys, woods and pastures offer predators resources unevenly distributed across the land. The approaches to the Old Forest, the edges of cultivated country and the minor roads of Minhiriath may preserve signs of wild animals. In difficult seasons, wolves may draw nearer to livestock or follow routes used by herbivores. Depopulated regions offer different conditions again: some places grow over with scrub, roads become less frequented and the movement of game changes. Encounters there do not therefore necessarily reveal a supernatural origin.

Yet you have learnt a rule according to which wolves cannot keep following a trail once it crosses any stretch of water, however shallow. The Baranduin, the branches of the Gwathló and the floodplains of Minhiriath supposedly form effective barriers, provided a traveller reaches the far bank before his pursuers. Trackers advocating the method claim that water removes all scent and that predators, deprived of bearings, always abandon any attempt to find a neighbouring ford. They therefore recommend fleeing towards channels, crossing a river at the first opportunity and letting the current destroy all signs of passage. This confidence overlooks wolves' ability to search the banks, cross certain waterways and recover a trail beyond an obstacle. It turns a real difficulty of the ground into infallible protection.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

Cardolan combines valley country, low hills, scattered woodland and lands shaped by former human settlement. Wolves may move through this country where they find game and places quiet enough to rest. Their presence depends more upon available resources and the possibilities afforded by the ground than upon the history of tombs or the proximity of ancient battlefields. Woodland may conceal their movements, but open country is not forbidden to them. Rivers and marshes complicate some crossings without always imposing an impassable boundary. A pack established in a wooded region may exploit neighbouring pastures, while a lone individual may cross a country without remaining there.

You know that a large print on a highway does not itself identify a wolf, and that a cry heard beside a mound does not establish the presence of a supernatural being. Ordinary animals do not recognise burial places as consecrated frontiers. Scrubland may shelter small game, and an abandoned estate may attract herbivores when human activity declines. Wolves may follow these resources, as may other predators and scavengers. When a story tells of a beast appearing amongst the barrows, it is important to distinguish what the witness described from the interpretation given to the encounter by local inhabitants. A natural wolf may indeed have passed, but this must be confirmed by evidence appropriate to the circumstances.

### Success — `success`

Cardolan demands a particular understanding of the relationship between waterways, ancient routes and abandoned landscapes. The valleys of the Baranduin and Gwathló may guide animal movement along practicable banks, but floods can rapidly change access to them. In the lowlands of Minhiriath, wetlands, woods and meadows do not everywhere offer equally easy passage. ### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

Roads and constructions used by Men may also be used by animals. ### Success — `success`

Rhudaur's heights and the regions of the Angle form environments whose constraints vary greatly through the year. Their presence follows not a simple opposition between mountain and valley but a succession of opportunities made favourable or unfavourable by local conditions. Ground suitable for hunting in autumn may lose its value in midwinter if game has moved away.

The neighbourhood of the two great rivers calls for closer observation. Wolves do not fear stone works for mysterious reasons. Inhabited regions add further considerations. Herds driven towards grazing lands attract predators where guarding becomes lax, while dogs, fences and nearby people impose real constraints. Inhabitants who have suffered Orc attacks or Warg raids are understandably inclined to fear every great canid. An observer who knows the passages between the hills and the Angle compares marks found on different surfaces, then asks whether the same animal could actually have left them in the time available. In a landscape burdened with ancient fears and wartime stories, this restraint marks a genuinely higher degree of knowledge. Angmar lies north of the old kingdoms of Arnor, amongst windswept lands, rugged heights where roads become scarce and the approaches to Carn Dûm. The Shire is a land of gentle hills, fields, hedges, copses and well-kept paths, where Hobbits know the seasons of the garden and the rhythms of the farm better than the movements of great predators.
