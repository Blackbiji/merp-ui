# Wolf — Racial Lore

## General: The Character’s Own Culture

*Specialisation: Own Culture · Family: Racial Lore · 5 RMU results · Literary Version 1 — For approval*

This table assumes no universal body of wolf lore shared by every people of Middle-earth. It describes how a character has inherited advice, stories, prohibitions and memories concerning the animal within his or her own community. Depending upon the country, those words may come from a shepherd, a tracker, a traveller, a family dwelling far from the forests or an elder preserving the memory of war. The degree of success measures the character’s understanding of this inheritance, not the extent of general zoological knowledge: lore particular to a region and direct observation belong to other fields.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

In the tales of your people, the appearance of a lone wolf means that an entire pack has already selected its prey. The elders teach that such animals show themselves before a great hunt, and that their howls carry word to the other members of the band.

A story often told during long night watches holds that a wolf appears alone only to test a traveller’s courage. Its stillness, its gaze and the distance it maintains are supposed to betray an unseen presence: the pack is said to be concealed out of sight, waiting for the traveller to take flight.

### Failure — `failure`

The hunters of your people pass down an old rule: a wolf that carries its tail low belongs to a roaming pack, whereas one that raises its tail is hunting alone. This difference of posture is said to be reliable enough to foretell whether other wolves are nearby.

Some elders maintain that careful observation of a wolf’s tail reveals its intentions as plainly as a man’s words. A lowered tail is supposed to announce a collective hunt and the nearness of other members of the group; an upright tail, by contrast, is believed to mark a beast travelling without companions.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

Your people know wolves through stories, warnings and customs handed down over generations. In stock-rearing communities, such accounts often accompany the evening gatherings and the advice given to young herdsmen. Their meaning depends as much upon the lives of those who tell them as upon the animal itself.

You recognise that the stories of your people fulfil several purposes. They teach children not to stray from a camp, remind herdsmen to bring their animals in before nightfall, and sometimes explain why a family abandoned a pasture. The wolf is a convenient figure in such tales, yet the lesson often concerns people more than the beast. Fear of the cold, the duty to aid a neighbour and the difficulty of protecting stores of food are given a form that is easy to remember. A story may therefore preserve a genuine old incident without providing an accurate guide to the behaviour of wolves.

### Success — `success`

You remember how the stories of your community distinguish the observations of shepherds and hunters from those tales intended chiefly to teach caution. Certain sayings repeated to children concern the perils of isolation, cold or disobedience more than the true behaviour of wolves. Mutual assistance between herdsmen and the protection of flocks occupy a more practical place in everyday custom.

You know how to seek the circumstances in which a custom first arose. A prohibition against going out alone may stem from a real attack, but equally from a winter in which children lost their way; a rule for guarding stock may persist long after wolves have vanished from the district. Similarly, some tales of enormous black beasts may have confused natural wolves with Wargs, or borrowed the language of warfare to describe events that were never part of ordinary wild life. A tradition derives its value from what it preserves of place, period and witness, not from the number of generations that repeat it.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You know the variants of the same tale or injunction, and the circumstances in which your people began to pass them down. The place given to the wolf may change according to whether the story concerns hunting, a warning to children or the memory of troubled times. In a saying apparently devoted to the animal, you can recognise a rule of conduct or a collective memory particular to your community, while keeping these meanings distinct from what observation of living creatures can establish.

Your knowledge allows you to trace a single story through its transformations. One valley may preserve the image of the wolf as a thief of livestock, a town that of a prowler waiting outside the gates, and a hunting country that of a competitor following great herbivores. None of these portraits encompasses the whole species, but each reveals the concerns of those who fashioned it. You distinguish words intended to enforce a social rule, the memory of a witnessed encounter, and a fable whose authority rests chiefly upon its beauty or power to stir the imagination. When several versions contradict one another, you use the differences to investigate their origins and purposes rather than choosing the most frightening. Such understanding does not teach you to count wolves in a forest. It teaches you what weight to give a community’s testimony, and why the people of one region speak of the animal in ways their neighbours might find strange.

# Wolf — Racial Lore

## Common Orcs — Yrch

*Specialisation: Common Orcs — Yrch · Family: Racial Lore · 5 RMU results · Literary Version 1 — For approval*

In the camps of the Yrch, the wolf stands at the border between several worlds: the beasts that come for scraps, the predators of the marches, and the creatures which captains boast of setting upon their enemies. Tales of plunder readily ascribe a disciplined ferocity to every large canid. The tradition thus inherited is inseparable from hunger, rivalry over spoils and the boasting of warriors. This table concerns knowledge circulating among ordinary Orcs, without confusing the natural wolf with the Warg or turning a habit of the camp into a law of nature.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

You know an old camp saying that a wolf which accepts food near Orcs will never bare its teeth against them. It is claimed that the smell of their fires and armour suffices to make the beast acknowledge its masters, even when no one has ever fed or trained it. The notion is so familiar to you that you might approach a wild animal attracted to the remains of a bivouac without fear, as though the mere nearness of your own kind compelled it to obey.

Young raiders are sometimes taught that a wolf which has tasted the refuse of a camp has unwittingly acknowledged its masters’ authority. From that moment, it will supposedly keep a respectful distance from the warriors who fed it and turn its violence instead upon travellers, enemy flocks or stray beasts.

### Failure — `failure`

Tales of raids have led you to believe that a long, deep howl always announces the arrival of other wolves, while a short cry means the animal has found prey. You readily interpret these sounds as signals of a discipline akin to that of armed bands. You might therefore attempt to predict a pack’s movements from this imagined code, without knowing whether the animal is calling its own kind, answering a distant voice or merely announcing its presence.

One raiders’ belief maintains that the precise purpose of a wolf’s cry can be read in its duration. Deep calls are said to gather hunters, brief cries to mark a victim, and silence to foretell an ambush.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that ordinary wolves are not all servants of those who fight beneath the Shadow. Some use the same passages as Orc bands because carcasses and other remains are available there, but this makes them neither tame nor capable of understanding orders. A wild beast may flee from warriors, dispute their prey or approach them when the promise of food outweighs its fear.

You know that proximity between the two kinds does not create an alliance. Wolves may visit an old battlefield because food remains there; they may also abandon a camp when a noisy company arrives, or compete over carrion with other animals. War displaces people, herds and game, temporarily opening opportunities for predators. A sentry who reports seeing eyes in the darkness may therefore have witnessed an animal’s presence, but his account alone reveals neither the creature’s identity nor its reason for being there.

### Success — `success`

You understand the difference between a free animal accustomed to taking advantage of an army’s waste and one kept in the service of a master. Tales of wolves employed in the northern wars do not turn every wolf encountered near a camp into a mount, sentry or ally. Beasts that sometimes follow warriors may be interested in their food rather than their quarrels; when frightened, hungry or wounded, their behaviour becomes especially unpredictable if one mistakenly assumes that they will obey.

You distinguish the fear wolves inspire from the means by which their behaviour may actually be influenced. An animal accustomed to easy scraps may return for several successive nights and keep less distance than a wolf driven from the valleys. Such familiarity may increase the danger rather than remove it. Warriors who throw bones beyond the firelight can thereby attract visitors whom they mistake for auxiliaries. Their anger when these beasts devour stores or threaten a mount reveals the limits of their supposed command.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You can readily distinguish what war happens to bring together from what it truly commands. Wolves may profit from the devastation left in an army’s wake and appear to be following its march, while other creatures of similar appearance belong to the darker histories of the Shadow and great wars. It is dangerous, however, to combine them in one category. The ordinary wolf responds to hunger, fear, territory and opportunities for food; its proximity to Orcs proves no allegiance. Tales that celebrate the supposedly natural mastery of beasts by Orc warbands often reveal more about the brutality and boasting of their narrators than about the behaviour of wolves.

In the memories of Orc bands, the same incident may change its meaning according to which victor tells it. A company followed by wolves becomes an army attended by its war-hounds; a beast that averts its gaze is transformed into a respectful servant; an attack upon isolated livestock becomes a blow struck against the enemy. You recognise the interests behind these accounts and separate them from observable facts: the direction of tracks, the availability of food, an animal’s fear of people and the possibility of natural dispersal. You also know that the Wargs of the great tales possess abilities and associations that cannot be inferred merely from the appearance of a large wolf. An ordinary pack does not become a military unit because it wanders amidst the wreckage of war. It swears no oath, observes no camp boundary and cannot be counted faithful to its supposed masters.

## Covsheknarod — Êluzan

*Specialisation: Covsheknarod — Êluzan · Family: Racial Lore · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary version 1 — For approval*

The Covsheknarod know the long tracks, the stages of a journey carefully reckoned and the hardships of travel whose success depends upon pack animals and reserves of water. Their tales of wolves chiefly come from routes crossing cooler or higher country than their customary lands, and from dealings with peoples who live beside the grazing grounds. In this tradition the animal is less an everyday presence than a figure in stories told by guides, caravan leaders and travellers recalling ill-chosen halts. The knowledge so acquired may be remarkably precise concerning tracks, yet limited in its understanding of the actual distribution of wolves in the South.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

Among the Covsheknarod tribes, whose caravans know how scarce watering places can be, some tales attribute to wolves of the northern lands an almost unfailing ability to find hidden springs. Their sense of smell is said to detect moisture deep beneath stone and sand, even in districts where no stream comes to the surface. Travellers are said to have found pools by following a predator's movements from afar, and this fortunate encounter has become, in tradition, proof of a gift common to the whole species. Guides who rely upon the idea regard wolf tracks as certain indications that water is near, even when the animals are crossing a country for quite different reasons. The memory of arid roads has given this belief particular force.

One caravan tale speaks of an exhausted man who followed a large canid through the night until its steps led him to a basin of water concealed among rocks. Through constant retelling, the chance encounter became the principle that wolves can always divine the location of springs, even when they lie buried beneath parched earth.

### Failure — `failure`

The peoples of Bozisha-Miraz know the animals which move around caravans, herds and resting places. Certain traditions maintain that wolves never approach a tightly gathered circle of pack animals, for the mingling scents and continual movement prevent them from singling out a victim. This confusion of their senses is supposedly strong enough to make them abandon the hunt, even when young or weakened beasts remain at the edge of the group. Accounts of long journeys completed without loss have lent comfortable authority to the rule. Gathering one's mounts together has thus come to be regarded as sufficient protection in itself, regardless of watchmen or other travelling precautions.

Other stories grant closely packed caravans a mysterious protection: the smell of the mounts, their crossing movements and the clatter of their bells supposedly make wolves unable to pick out prey. Even a young animal falling behind would therefore be safe by virtue of the nearby baggage alone, without either a watchman or a dog.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

Among the Covsheknarod, wolves are chiefly known from caravan tales and contacts with countries less arid than their tribal territories. You know that these predators principally seek animal prey and that their presence depends upon sufficient resources, particularly game and water. They may follow routes used by other beasts without wishing to approach men, just as they may take advantage of carcasses left beside a road. The wisest guides distinguish a wolf's track from reliable information concerning springs or routes. Animals follow their own needs; they share neither the aims nor the maps of travellers.

You know that an animal's trail does not disclose its destination. A wolf may be approaching a familiar watering place, seeking prey or simply crossing a valley before turning elsewhere. In regions where drought restricts the presence of large herbivores, wolves themselves are not necessarily established; stories brought from the North do not prove their abundance at southern stopping places. Useful trackers distinguish what a passage actually reveals from what they would like it to mean, and do not commit a caravan to the promise of a spring without further evidence.

### Success — `success`

The knowledge of trails taught by caravan leaders allows a more accurate understanding of wolves' movements. You know that these animals may travel considerable distances between favourable places and recognise sites they have visited before, but possess no infallible sense for discovering underground water. They may turn towards a valley because game passes through it, because they know it already, or because the route demands less effort. Herds of livestock do not necessarily prevent them from recognising an opportunity to hunt, especially when one beast becomes separated from its companions. Attentive Covsheknarod guards therefore watch their mounts' movements as carefully as the tracks upon the ground, knowing that vigilance cannot be replaced by a single rule of the road.

Long-distance travel teaches the comparison of observations separated by time. A series of prints may belong to an animal temporarily using the same road as a caravan without following the men themselves; a carcass attracts scavengers whose movements are sometimes mistaken for a pursuit. You know to seek signs of repeated use rather than regard one trail as a map. You also understand why protecting a herd never depends upon one device: the animals should indeed be gathered together, but they also require attentive guards, a well-chosen resting place and precautions adjusted to changing ground.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

The life of the Covsheknarod demands unremitting attention to distances, water and whatever resources the country can provide. You know that wolves, when they inhabit lands where these needs are difficult to meet, likewise adapt their movements without imitating the organisation of a caravan. They may travel with endurance, use familiar watering places and follow game along paths crossing those of men. Their sense of smell and memory contribute to such abilities, but no single track reveals the existence of a hidden spring. Similarly, domestic animals are best protected by a combination of measures and an assessment of local risks, not merely by gathering them into a group. In stories of southern roads, the most trustworthy observers distinguish fortunate coincidences that have become proverbial from habits genuinely observed more than once. Thus the skill of a guide, like that of a hunter, is measured by what he can recognise and by the certainties he refuses to draw from too few signs.

The finest Covsheknarod accounts distinguish a happy accident from a method that can be taught. A traveller may truly have discovered water after following a wolf without the beast having been able to sense a buried spring from a great distance. A caravan may have crossed country without attack because natural resources sufficed for the predators, because men stood watch or because no wolves were present; those explanations cannot be established from the outcome of the journey alone. You know how to place accounts within the circumstances of season, terrain and animal life. Tradition can then keep its beauty without becoming dangerous advice: it may inspire caution, but cannot replace checking the ground, the wells and the circumstances actually observed.

## Daen Lintis — Dunlendings

*Specialisation: Daen Lintis — Dunlendings · Family: Racial Lore · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary version 1 — For approval*

For the Daen clans, the wolf moves through memories of grazing lands, conflicts with neighbours and great gatherings upon the heights of Dunland. Its tracks cross those of herds; its cries are sometimes heard beyond the ridges dividing one holding from another. The elders see in these landscapes boundaries charged with oaths and memories, though wild beasts know nothing of them. This table explores how a people accustomed to defending their land may transform the observation of a predator into a moral rule, an accusation or the memory of a clan.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

The oral traditions of the Daen clans maintain that wolves respect the ancient ridgelines separating pastures and hunting grounds. Beasts established on one side of a height are said seldom to return from the other, as though they too recognised the boundaries laid down by the ancestors through oaths and feuds. Shepherds cite valleys where the same howling was heard for years without an attack ever taking place in the neighbouring country. This apparent constancy has fostered a belief familiar to upland communities: wolves supposedly remain bound to the limits of their pack's territory almost as faithfully as a clan to its land. An ancient ridge is therefore regarded as natural protection of predictable duration.

Tradition holds that packs choose their ridges as families choose their holdings. An ancient line of stones, a pass used by one clan alone or the summit of a height regarded as inviolable supposedly becomes, for wolves as well, the boundary of a territory inherited by the young from their parents.

### Failure — `failure`

Gatherings of the Dunland clans sometimes leave such evidence of footsteps, smoke and hearths that the sites are credited with particular safety from wolves. Some herdsmen assert that predators avoid ground where large numbers of men have stayed together for a long time, even after the tents have been folded and the livestock taken elsewhere. They explain this caution as a memory of the collective strength which wolves have supposedly learned to fear. Stories of flocks left undisturbed near an old meeting place have made the belief an honoured precaution. Where families once assembled before their chiefs, the human presence is considered to remain impressed upon the scent of the soil long after the last voices and fires have faded.

It is also said that land marked by a great clan gathering remains feared by wolves for many years. Human footprints, fire-ash and the smell of horses are supposed to preserve the memory of a multitude which predators continue to dread after the last guards have departed.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

In the highlands of the Daen Lintis, wolves belong to the history of grazing lands, ridge paths and long winters when herds struggle to find enough food. You know that these animals chiefly follow available resources and that their presence can vary greatly from valley to valley. They generally avoid confronting people, but never acknowledge clan boundaries or human ownership. Old herdsmen distinguish a predator's passage from the disappearance of a beast and remind others that flocks may scatter through fear or bad weather. In a society where one loss may inflame rivalry between families, this distinction is knowledge as valuable as a hunter's skill.

You know that a valley occupied by livestock and another left to wild game may offer very different opportunities to wolves. Their presence depends upon resources, resting places and seasons, not the antiquity of human feuds. In country of wet slopes and narrow passages, an animal can disappear from a shepherd's sight without crossing any notable frontier. Above all, you understand that seeing a wolf near a pen does not prove that it made an earlier attack: the loss of a sheep requires specific evidence before one clan accuses another or begins a retaliatory hunt.

### Success — `success`

Daen clan memory preserves many tales of animals which went astray and were too readily blamed upon wolves, before it emerged that they had fallen, succumbed to illness or been moved with a flock by neighbours. You know that footprints, the remains of a meal and signs of struggle do not all point to the same cause. Wolves may follow ridges to reach an easy crossing, then descend towards valleys holding game; their territory does not coincide with human boundaries. Wild animals may also return to a place long neglected when favourable circumstances arise. The most respected trackers therefore examine the facts before naming a culprit, knowing that a clan's anger, once awakened, can prove harder to turn aside than a hungry predator.

Trails must be considered in the light of the ground. A ridge path affords a view of several slopes, but descending into a valley gives access to prey and water; a wolf may use both in a single day. Keeping flocks together, maintaining fences and posting guards matter more than the reputation of an ancient council ground. You distinguish a fresh trail from a print altered by runoff and do not treat every dead animal as the certain victim of an attack. In the highlands such care may settle a quarrel before it gathers the force of a vow of vengeance.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

Daen Lintis traditions intertwine the memory of their people with that of the lands they inhabit. You know that a tale of wolves may preserve the recollection of a deadly winter, a quarrel over pastures or a courageous watch, without portraying the animals' behaviour with equal accuracy. Wolves remain opportunistic predators whose families respond to food, breeding and the needs of their territory rather than the divisions of the clans. Their movements may cross several holdings in one season, and a quiet valley does not prove that another people has driven the beasts away. The wisest observers distinguish signs upon the ground from interpretations inherited from old conflicts. In these highlands, where a given word carries considerable weight, it is sometimes harder to correct an ancestral story than to recognise a fresh trail; nevertheless, a hunter's first loyalty must be to the facts.

Daen tradition often preserves several versions of the same loss. The shepherd describes the beast torn from the flock; his neighbour recalls poorly fastened gates; the chief remembers the winter when hunting had to give way to guarding the families. You know how to compare these voices with the landscape and seek what the evidence can still establish. Wolves may frequent slopes where their prey gathers, then abandon them as conditions change. A pack follows neither the loyalty of a clan nor its political memory: it may cross several human territories and reappear in a valley long silent. The highest knowledge therefore lies in separating the honour of men from the behaviour of animals, without despising the practical warnings handed down by earlier generations.

## Drúedain / Woses — Drughu

*Specialisation: Drúedain / Woses — Drughu · Family: Racial Lore · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary version 1 — For approval*

In the memory of the Drughu, the smallest things in a wood are not without significance: the bending of a blade of grass, a broken stem or the freshness of a footprint may reveal that an animal has passed unseen. The wolf is regarded as a neighbour in the living world, to be studied attentively rather than treated as an omen invariably charged with supernatural intent. This table acknowledges the woodland knowledge of the Drúedain without presenting all their peoples as possessors of identical learning. Above all, it distinguishes their skill in reading signs made by people from the independent responses of animals.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

Among the hunting tales of the Drúedain, some credit wolves with so lasting a memory of human scents that an animal which has crossed a fresh trail will never return to that same path. The presence of a man supposedly remains there as a sign of danger even after he has gone. Elders who uphold this opinion tell of wolves seen only once near a track and never again observed there at the same hour. Their absence has been interpreted as a deliberate decision to avoid that route thereafter. In traditions belonging to people skilled at concealing their own passage, this supposed faculty of predators is held to be particularly useful: a clearly marked human trail is thought sufficient to divert a wolf permanently from any path it crosses.

A hunting tale recounts how a wolf turned aside after crossing the trail of a Drughu. The story concludes that human scent provides lasting protection against predators: once warned that a path is used by men, a wolf will supposedly never venture along it again.

### Failure — `failure`

Another Drughu belief holds that wolves recognise the marks a hunter carves into a trunk or stone and attach to them the same meaning as the scent warnings of their own kind. Such signs, intended at first for people, would eventually become part of the forest's silent language. Predators would visit a territory less often if its entrances were marked in this fashion, particularly where the signs appeared along several paths. The tradition seems all the more persuasive because the Drúedain themselves can read the faintest clues left in the wood. Their hunting tales therefore invest these marks with a meaning extending beyond those who have learnt their use, as though a notch cut by a human hand could be understood instinctively by a wolf.

A further tradition attributes to marks carved into tree trunks a power beyond their use among hunters. Notches placed at certain heights are supposedly understood by wolves as territorial warnings, as unmistakable as the scents through which the animals communicate with one another.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

The traditions of the Drúedain give animals a place among the many signs through which a forest makes itself known. Wolves are recognised as skilful hunters whose habits influence the routes taken by game and the hours when certain clearings remain undisturbed. You know that they feed chiefly upon wild prey and that their presence does not in itself reveal any hostile design against people. A solitary footprint, a displaced branch or a distant cry cannot disclose the whole course of a hunt. The most experienced Drughu place greater trust in several indications which agree than in the account of a startled witness. Such caution grows from a patient reading of the earth, fallen leaves and the movements of animals.

You know that the signs left by animals acquire meaning only in relation to the ground upon which they appear. A single track in wet earth does not disclose every intention of its maker, and the memory of a path once avoided does not prove that it will remain so. The Drughu may pass on the habit of bending to examine details that others trample underfoot, but such attentiveness licenses no magical interpretation. Wolves may catch the scent of a man and decide to withdraw; they may equally return when hunger, the passage of game or a change of wind presents another opportunity.

### Success — `success`

The knowledge of Drughu trackers rests upon comparing tracks, their condition and the circumstances in which they were made. You know that a wolf can distinguish a multitude of scents and may follow a human trail when it offers something of interest, rather than being held at bay merely because it has encountered one. Its movements depend upon game, convenient passages and the needs of its pack; signs carved by people carry no meaning that the animal would naturally understand. The most attentive Drúedain know how to distinguish marks left by a claw, paw or rubbing body from ordinary damage to bark. They also watch the reactions of other animals, whose alarm may indicate a presence without revealing its source with certainty.

You understand why a mark must never be read in isolation. The depth of a footprint in mud, the direction in which leaves have been flattened and the remains of prey may disclose a sequence of events; a gust of wind or fresh rain may erase some of its stages. A wolf may take a human path because it offers easy travelling, even while remaining capable of avoiding places where people have settled. You can appreciate this seeming contradiction and warn your companions without promising an illusory safety. What remains effective is the watch kept over approaches, adjusted to the actual evidence, not the carving of a sign.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

Within the heritage of the Drúedain, the protection of their own people often rests upon knowledge of the living world precise enough to make displays of force unnecessary. You know that wolves acquire certain habits through experience and generally live in family groups whose members cooperate. Their routes may remain regular while a territory supplies food and shelter, then change when the seasons, the game or the presence of people alters. Nothing obliges them, however, to respect the marks of another people or to recognise an intention behind every trace. The traditions of watch-stones and protective signs belong to the history of the Drughu; they must not be confused with the sensory language of animals. The wisest observers perceive an essential distinction here: knowing the forest does not mean supposing that it thinks like its speaking inhabitants, but recognising the necessities proper to each living creature.

The most subtle Drughu knowledge does not turn beasts into compliant symbols of the people's own arts. You can recognise patterns without treating them as laws: a path may seem to be used chiefly before dawn, yet its use may change with the season; a wolf family may avoid a clearing for a time, yet its territory does not end at the circle of human fires. The marks left by hunters remain messages among their own people, not commands issued to the forest. When old stories speak of a beast that understood a warning, you ask what concrete signs could have been observed, what gesture was interpreted and how memory transformed it. This distinction preserves the subtlety of the traditions without attributing to them a power they do not always claim to possess.

## Black Númenóreans

*Specialisation: Black Númenóreans · Family: Racial Lore · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary version 1 — For approval*

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that natural wolves are not soldiers and must not be confused with Wargs. They may approach camps for food, remain cautious before noise or return after troops leave. Receiving scraps does not give an animal loyalty to a garrison; it chiefly follows resources available to it. Military signs and Olog-hai emblems impose no general authority upon a wild pack.

You also know that wolves howl to communicate with one another, not to deliver coded reports to soldiers. The calls' intensity, apparent number and sequence do not automatically reveal an enemy movement. Warriors who wish to know what approaches must question sentries, seek tracks and distinguish ordinary animals from intelligent creatures which may genuinely have taken part in the wars of the Shadow.

### Success — `success`

Garrison experience has taught you how military campaigns alter the resources available to predators. Carcasses, lost pack animals and abandoned remains may draw wolves after soldiers have passed. Conversely, noisy occupation, dogs and the destruction of game may make certain places less favourable. A wolf appearing over several nights near the same refuse store may have learnt that food can be found there, without seeking to serve the occupants. The habit may disappear or become dangerous when circumstances change.

You also know that animals do not communicate through an army's codes. Howling helps maintain contact within a family, signal presence and sometimes answer other wolves. Slopes and wind alter its apparent range. A series of calls need not indicate several packs; silence proves neither the absence of animals nor that of an enemy company. Military reconnaissance demands other observations, independent of noises heard beyond a camp.

Finally, you distinguish the natural wolf from the Warg without assuming size or coat colour gives the answer. A creature capable of understanding orders, pursuing a tactical design or deliberately cooperating with troops requires a different enquiry. Natural wolves may hunt together, but their family cooperation is not a cavalry unit. Wise officers do not attribute the achievements or responsibilities of the Shadow's armies to beasts drawn by the remains of fighting.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

You understand that the Olog-hai's power of command extends over troops subject to orders, not every creature frequenting Mordor's lands. Natural wolves respond to their needs, the presence of game and experience acquired near Men and Orcs. Proximity to a fortress may be explained by accessible resources or practicable passages. Even when an individual appears to recognise a camp's routine, it does not understand its emblems or objectives. Familiarity with food creates habits, and sometimes risks, without producing political loyalty.

You know how to examine tracks without turning them into patrol reports. A trail near a roadway may belong to a wolf following game; several prints beside a refuse store may reflect successive visits. Rain, ground and overlapping marks prevent the exact number of animals being inferred from one sign. Likewise, howling in a valley may echo between slopes and create a false impression of nearness or multitude. A source is trustworthy only when its circumstances are understood.

You also understand how military memories sometimes distort the nature of predators. One officer may call Wargs which truly took part in an operation 'wolves'; another may describe scavengers appearing behind a column after battle as an escort. The accounts need not refer to the same sort of creature. You seek deeds showing intelligence, speech or deliberate cooperation before speaking of supernatural auxiliaries. A captain's name for an animal is not evidence enough.

Finally, natural laws have no obligation to follow Mordor's hierarchy. A wolf family may avoid a garrison, profit from its remains or cross the lands it watches according to season and resources. Dispersing animals may appear without belonging to a nearby pack. Complete knowledge separates these movements from the command of troops and reserves Wargs their proper place where their presence is truly established. No order spoken in the Black Speech offers a universal guarantee of safety from a natural wolf.

# Wolf — Racial Lore

## Half-Trolls — Pertorog / Pirtereg

*Specialisation: Half-Trolls — Pertorog / Pirtereg · Culture key: `culture-half-trolls` · Family: Racial Lore · 5 RMU outcomes · Literary Version 1 — For Review*

Half-Troll traditions may bring together experiences of warfare, watching roads and living amongst very different communities. Physical strength occupies an important place in their stories, but confers no hereditary power over animals. Natural wolves may be met near camps, on the marches or in country where game and pastures offer resources; they know nothing of the duties or lineages Half-Trolls claim amongst the peoples of the Shadow. This table examines knowledge of the terrain and the errors born of projecting relationships of dominance onto wolf families, whilst preserving the intelligent and malevolent nature of Wargs as a separate matter.

### Absolute Failure — `absoluteFailure`

Some Half-Troll captains teach that wolves instinctively recognise the ancestry of the Pertorog and Pirtereg. Their sense of smell supposedly detects a strength akin to that of the Black Trolls, and the animal refuses to attack a warrior bearing those marks. A single encounter would suffice for the wolf to understand that it faced a being born to command rather than prey. Elders describe this response as a respect deeper than ordinary fear of a formidable adversary.

Young warriors are therefore advised never to turn away before a pack, but to advance calmly with their weapons visible. Wolves, the veterans insist, recognise such assurance and withdraw from the road even if they have never met the traveller. A Half-Troll would need neither to worry about provisions left nearby nor to look for fresh tracks: his very presence would compel beasts to keep a proper distance. The strength of his blood would suffice to make a safe passage through wild country.

### Failure — `failure`

Another belief gives wolves irrevocable loyalty towards whoever defeats them by force. A Half-Troll capable of scattering a pack and putting its strongest animal to flight would be recognised as its master until his death. The beasts would remember the defeat and could thereafter neither disobey his orders nor attack his companions. Dominance won in a single encounter would become a lasting bond, independent of food, territory or circumstance.

Elders sometimes speak of warriors followed for several days by wolves which did not approach them. These stories are interpreted as proof that the animals obeyed their conqueror and guarded his rear. They teach that repeating the same gestures of intimidation when a new group appears is enough to make every neighbouring pack acknowledge the Half-Troll's authority. He could then travel with an escort of wild predators without feeding or training them, for the first fear would have become loyalty.

### Partial Success — `partialSuccess`

You know that natural wolves may be cautious of a large adversary without recognising his ancestry or granting him rank. Their behaviour depends upon hunger, fear, available prey and experience. A pack may withdraw before a powerful company, but that does not mean it will obey the company later. Half-Trolls possess no natural right over predators inhabiting the regions through which they travel.

You also know that animals may follow a camp for its refuse, avoid a patrol or cross a road when it becomes quiet. Such encounters prove neither submission nor alliance. Intelligent Wargs belong to another category and may sometimes maintain relationships with troops of the Shadow; this does not turn natural wolves into guards or mounts for those who defeated them in battle.

### Success — `success`

Experience of the marches has taught you that frightening a predator is not the same as training it. A wolf stepping back may be avoiding injury; it may return once food becomes accessible or danger seems diminished. Animals need not memorise a human or Trollish hierarchy to adapt their conduct. The same gestures will not therefore always have the same result, particularly when an individual is startled, wounded or accustomed to finding refuse near camps. You know that vigilance and proper care of supplies remain essential.

You also understand wolves' family life. A pack's cooperation arises chiefly from the bonds between its members and their need for food, not a succession of duels for command. An animal seen at the head of a trail is not necessarily a chief. Members may separate, rejoin or leave their territory; a dispersing young wolf may travel great distances without forming a troop. Tales of victors securing an escort by strength alone project warriors' rules onto these relationships.

Finally, you distinguish wild animals from Wargs rightly mentioned in certain military traditions. Intelligence capable of understanding commitments, following orders or acting within a hostile enterprise must not be inferred from a wolf following camp scraps. Witnesses may confuse the categories, especially in darkness or after a fight. A careful observer attends to the conduct actually seen rather than the supposed power of whoever recounts the story.

### Absolute Success — `absoluteSuccess`

Your understanding of wolves depends upon neither birth nor martial renown. A pack is generally a family whose members cooperate to find resources and rear young; its relationships do not imitate military discipline. A Half-Troll may be a dangerous adversary, but his strength gives him no lasting dominion over animals. Wolves may avoid a place where they have been at risk and return when circumstances change. Their behaviour is flexible enough to make every rule of absolute submission misleading.

Observations of the land offer more dependable knowledge than tales of victory. A trail may run beside a camp because game uses the same passage; carcasses left after a march may draw scavengers; several prints near a ford do not prove an organised group watching travellers. You compare datable tracks, accessible resources and independent observations before suggesting an explanation. One wolf's presence warrants neither counting an invisible pack nor attributing to it a desire to pursue anyone.

You also understand the limits of acquired habits. An animal fed repeatedly may seek to return without becoming loyal to the person who gave it meat. Repeated proximity to people may change its caution and sometimes increase the dangers of an encounter. Conversely, a frightened wolf may remain away for a long time without acknowledging a hierarchy. The circumstances of an interaction matter more than the strength of a lineage. You advise vigilance and the safeguarding of stores rather than a display of dominance.

Finally, you interpret wartime traditions concerning Wargs separately from the existence of ordinary wolves. The former may describe intelligent creatures capable of alliance and malice; the latter follow their needs and experience without sharing the ambitions of peoples they encounter. Great size, dark fur or an appearance near a garrison do not suffice to determine the animal's nature. Complete Half-Troll knowledge therefore consists not in seeing subjects to conquer everywhere, but in distinguishing the living world from the violent hierarchies some would impose upon it.
